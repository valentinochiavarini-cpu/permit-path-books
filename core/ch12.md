# Chapter 12: Your Car, Seat Belts and Passengers

You can be a careful, skilled driver and still get hurt by a bald tire, a burned out brake light or a seat belt worn the wrong way. Your car is part of your safety plan. So is the way every person in it is buckled in.

The knowledge test asks about this in two ways. Some questions check that you know how to keep a car safe to drive: tires, lights, mirrors and the warning lights on the dashboard. Others check the rules for protecting people: seat belts, airbags and child seats. On the road test, the examiner will often ask you to show the horn, the lights and the signals before you even leave the lot.

Seat belt and child seat laws are set by each state, so your state's exact rules appear in the boxes in this chapter. The safety advice around them comes from national crash research and holds everywhere.

>
> - How to wear a seat belt and why airbags do not replace it.
> - Where children should ride and which seat fits which child.
> - What to check before you drive: tires, lights, mirrors, windows.
> - What red and yellow dashboard warning lights mean.
> - Why you must never leave a child or pet alone in a car.
> - What papers you need in the car: license, registration and proof of insurance.

## 12.1 Before You Drive

A quick check before you drive catches most problems while they are still easy to fix. It takes a minute and it becomes a habit fast. Examiners on the road test watch for it.

### Walk Around the Car

Before you get in, walk around the car and look:

- **Tires:** do any look low or flat? Any bulges or deep cuts in the sidewalls?
- **Under the car:** fresh puddles of fluid can mean a leak.
- **Glass and lights:** are the windows, mirrors and light covers clean and unbroken?
- **Around the car:** children, pets, bikes or toys behind or beside you, especially before backing.

### Inside, Before You Start

1. Lock the doors.
2. Adjust the seat so you can press the brake pedal all the way down with your knee still slightly bent, and reach the top of the wheel with a slight bend in your elbows.
3. Adjust the head restraint so its middle is about level with the tops of your ears. Set too low, it cannot stop your head from snapping back in a rear end crash.
4. Set the inside mirror and both side mirrors (see 12.4).
5. Fasten your seat belt and make sure every passenger does the same.
6. Clear windows of frost, fog, snow and clutter. Never drive while looking through a small hole scraped in the ice.

> **Remember it:** Seat, head restraint, mirrors, belt. Set them in that order, every time, before the key turns.

## 12.2 Dashboard Warning Lights

When you start the car, many dashboard lights glow for a second or two as the car tests itself, then go out. A light that stays on or comes on while you are driving is telling you something. Your owner's manual shows every symbol your car uses.

The colors follow a common pattern:

| Color | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Serious problem or danger | Stop safely soon and check |
| Yellow or amber | Something needs attention | Have it checked soon |
| Green or blue | A system is on | Nothing; it is information |

Lights you should know:

- **Oil pressure** (red, shaped like an oil can): the engine may not be getting oil. Pull over and turn off the engine. Driving on can destroy it.
- **Engine temperature** (red, a thermometer in liquid): the engine is overheating. Pull over and let it cool. Never open a hot radiator cap.
- **Brake system** (red, often the word BRAKE or an exclamation point in a circle): the parking brake is on, or the brake system has a problem.
- **Battery or charging** (red, a battery shape): the charging system is failing and the car may soon stall.
- **Check engine** (yellow, an engine outline): have the engine checked soon. A flashing check engine light is more urgent.
- **Tire pressure** (yellow, a horseshoe shape with an exclamation point): one or more tires are low.
- **ABS** (yellow): the anti-lock brakes have a fault. Normal braking still works, but without the anti-lock help.
- A **blue** light shaped like a headlight means your high beams are on.

## 12.3 Tires, Brakes and Steering

### Tires

Your tires are the only parts of the car that touch the road, and each one touches an area about the size of your hand.

- **Pressure.** Use the pressure printed on the sticker inside the driver's door frame or in the owner's manual. The number on the tire's sidewall is the maximum the tire can hold, not the right pressure for your car. Check pressure when the tires are cold, at least once a month. Low tires wear out faster, waste fuel, handle poorly and can overheat and fail.
- **Tread.** Tread grooves push water out from under the tire. Worn tread raises your risk of skidding and hydroplaning in rain. An easy test uses a penny: slide it upside down into a groove. When the whole of Lincoln's head stays visible, the grooves are too shallow and it is time for a new tire. Most tires also have small bars molded across the grooves; when the tread wears down level with them, replace the tire.
- **Damage.** Bulges, cuts and objects stuck in the tread can lead to a blowout.

### Brakes

Watch for a brake pedal that feels soft or sinks toward the floor, a car that pulls to one side when you brake, or grinding and squealing sounds. Any of these means the brakes need a mechanic soon.

### Steering

The steering wheel should not have much free play, and the car should go straight on a level road without pulling. Shaking in the wheel can mean a tire or alignment problem.

## 12.4 Lights, Glass and Mirrors

### Lights

Check that your headlights (low and high beams), taillights, brake lights, turn signals and license plate light all work. Ask a friend to stand behind the car while you press the brake and use each signal, or check the reflection in a store window. A burned out brake light means the driver behind you gets no warning when you stop. A turn signal that blinks much faster than usual often means one of its bulbs has burned out.

Keep the lenses clean. Dirt and snow can cut the light from your headlights sharply, and you will not notice from inside the car.

### Windshield and Wipers

Keep the windshield clean inside and out. Replace wiper blades that streak or chatter, and keep the washer fluid full. A cracked windshield can block your view, and a chip in your line of sight can spread.

### Mirrors

Set the **inside mirror** to frame the whole rear window. Set each **side mirror** so you just barely see the side of your own car at the inner edge; this cuts down the blind spot. Even with good settings, every car has **blind spots**: areas beside and behind you that no mirror shows. Always turn your head and look over your shoulder before you change lanes or pull out. Chapter 6 covers blind spots in detail.

The passenger side mirror on most cars is convex and says OBJECTS IN MIRROR ARE CLOSER THAN THEY APPEAR. Vehicles in it are nearer than they look.

## 12.5 Other Equipment

### Keep the Car Tidy

Loose items fly in a crash or a hard stop. A water bottle that rolls under the brake pedal can keep you from stopping. Keep the floor near the pedals clear and put heavy items in the trunk.

Do not hang things from the inside mirror or stick items on the windshield where they block your view. Never let a passenger ride in the cargo area of a pickup or the back of an SUV.

> **Road tip:** Carry a small emergency kit: flashlight, warning triangles or flares, a first aid kit, jumper cables, a phone charger, water, and a blanket in winter. Know where your spare tire and jack are before you need them.

## 12.6 Seat Belts

Seat belts are the single best way to survive a crash. They keep you in your seat, spread the force of the crash across the strongest parts of your body, and keep you from being thrown out of the car. Being thrown from a vehicle is almost always deadly.

### Why "I'll Brace Myself" Does Not Work

In a crash at 30 mph, your body keeps moving at 30 mph after the car stops. Your arms cannot hold back that force. Without a belt, you hit the steering wheel, the windshield or the people in front of you, or you leave the car through a window. Most crashes happen close to home at ordinary speeds, so every trip counts, even a short one.

### Wearing It Right

- The **lap belt** goes low and snug across your hips, not your stomach.
- The **shoulder belt** crosses the middle of your chest and your shoulder, away from your neck.
- Never put the shoulder belt behind your back or under your arm. In a crash, that can cause serious internal injuries.
- Remove slack. A loose belt lets you slide forward before it catches you.
- Sit upright. Leaning the seat far back lets you slide under the belt.
- If you are pregnant, keep wearing the belt: shoulder belt between the breasts and off to the side of the belly, lap belt below the belly across the hips.

### The Law

Every state except New Hampshire requires adults in the front seat to wear a seat belt, and every state has belt or child seat rules for young passengers. In some states, an officer can stop you only for not wearing a belt (**primary enforcement**). In others, the officer can ticket you for it only after stopping you for something else (**secondary enforcement**). As the driver, you are usually responsible for making sure young passengers are buckled up.

## 12.7 Airbags

**Airbags** inflate in a fraction of a second in a moderate or severe crash. They add protection, but they are built to work with seat belts, not instead of them. An unbelted person can be thrown forward into an airbag as it bursts open, and that can cause serious injury or death.

- Sit with at least 10 inches between your chest and the airbag cover in the steering wheel. Move your seat back if you need to.
- Hold the wheel with your hands on either side, not across the top, so your arms are not thrown into your face if the airbag fires.
- Many cars also have side and curtain airbags. Do not lean your head against the door or window, and keep children from sleeping against it.
- A rear-facing child seat must never ride in a seat with an active front airbag.

## 12.8 Children and Passengers

### Where Children Ride

The back seat is the safest place for children. Keep children 12 and under in the back seat, away from the force of a front airbag.

### The Right Seat for the Child

A child moves through these stages as they grow. Keep each child in each stage as long as the seat's height and weight limits allow, rather than moving up early.

| Stage | Who | Key rule |
|---|---|---|
| Rear-facing seat | Infants and toddlers | Back seat; never facing an active airbag |
| Forward-facing seat with harness | Children who outgrow rear-facing | Use the harness and the top tether |
| Booster seat | Children who outgrow the harness | Until the adult belt fits right |
| Seat belt | Children the belt fits | Lap belt on upper thighs, shoulder belt on chest |

A child is ready for the adult belt alone when they can sit all the way back with knees bent at the seat edge, the lap belt lies across the upper thighs (not the stomach), and the shoulder belt crosses the chest and shoulder (not the neck or face). For many children that happens somewhere between 8 and 12 years old.

Install every car seat exactly as its instructions and your car's manual say. A seat that moves more than an inch side to side at the belt path is too loose. Many fire stations, hospitals and certified technicians check car seats for free.

### Never Leave a Child Alone in the Car

A closed car heats up fast, and a child's body heats up three to five times faster than an adult's. Cracking a window or parking in the shade does little to help. Children die in hot cars every year, and more than half of those deaths happen because a caregiver forgot the child was there.

- Never leave a child alone in a car, not even for a minute.
- Look in the back seat every time before you lock the car and walk away.
- Lock your car when it is parked so children cannot climb in and get trapped.
- If you see a child alone in a hot car, call 911 right away.

The same rule protects pets, which cannot cool themselves well either.

> **Remember it:** Look before you lock. Check the back seat every single time.

### Your Passengers Are Your Responsibility

As the driver, you set the rules in your car: everyone buckled before the car moves, no passengers in seats without belts, and no more passengers than there are belts. If a passenger is distracting you, pull over and settle it before you drive on. New drivers often face limits on how many passengers they may carry; Chapter 1 covers your state's limits.

## 12.9 License, Registration and Insurance

When you drive, keep these with you or in the car:

- Your **driver's license** or learner's permit.
- The vehicle's **registration**, which shows the car is registered with the state.
- **Proof of insurance**, on a card or, in most states, on your phone.

A police officer can ask to see all three at any traffic stop.

### Insurance

Nearly every state requires you to carry **liability insurance**, which pays for injuries and damage you cause to other people. Driving without it can lead to fines, a suspended license and having to pay for the damage yourself. Liability insurance does not pay to fix your own car; that takes collision coverage, which is optional unless a lender requires it. Each state sets its own minimum coverage amounts.

### Registration and Plates

Your car must be registered in your state, and the license plates must be clean, lit at night and easy to read. Renew the registration before it expires. If you move or sell the car, follow your state's rules for updating the record.

> **Test trap:** A test question may ask what the registration proves. It proves the car is registered with the state. It does not prove you are insured or licensed; those need their own documents.

## Quick Review

- Do a walk around and set seat, head restraint, mirrors and belt before every drive.
- Red dashboard lights mean stop soon and check; yellow means have it checked soon.
- Use the tire pressure on the door sticker, not the number on the tire.
- Worn tread raises the risk of skids and hydroplaning; replace worn tires.
- Wear the lap belt low on the hips and the shoulder belt across the chest, never behind you.
- Airbags work with seat belts, not instead of them; sit at least 10 inches back.
- Children 12 and under ride in the back; a rear-facing seat never goes in front of a live airbag.
- Keep each child in each seat stage until they reach that seat's limits.
- Never leave a child alone in a car; look before you lock.
- Carry your license, registration and proof of insurance.
