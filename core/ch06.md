# Chapter 6: Speed and Space

Most crashes are not caused by a car doing something strange. They happen because a driver is going too fast for what is ahead, or is too close to the car in front, and runs out of time. Speed and space are the two things you control every second you drive, and they decide how much time you have when something goes wrong.

The knowledge test checks both. You will see questions on the basic speed law, on what a posted limit really means, on how long it takes to stop, and on how far back you should follow. These questions often use a short scene: rain, a truck ahead, a car riding your bumper. If you understand why the rule exists, you can answer any version of the question.

On the road test, the examiner watches the same things. Rolling too fast into a turn, following too close or changing lanes without a head check costs points or ends the test on the spot.

> **On the test**
>
> - Know the basic speed law: never faster than is safe for conditions, even under the posted limit.
> - Know that driving too slowly can also be against the law.
> - Name the three parts of stopping distance and what makes each one longer.
> - Measure following distance in seconds, and know when to add more.
> - Know where your blind spots are and how to check them.
> - Know what to do when someone tailgates you.

## 6.1 The Basic Speed Law

Every state has some version of the **basic speed law**: you may never drive faster than is reasonable and safe for the conditions at that moment. Conditions include weather, light, traffic, the road surface, curves, hills, people walking nearby and how well you can see.

This law sits on top of every posted number. A Speed Limit sign of 45 tells you the most you may drive when the road is dry, traffic is light and you can see well. It does not tell you that 45 is safe tonight in heavy rain. If an officer decides your speed was too fast for the conditions, you can get a ticket while your speedometer reads 38.

Think of it this way: the posted limit is a ceiling, and the conditions decide how far below the ceiling you need to be.

Slow down below the limit when:

- Rain, snow, ice, fog, smoke or dust is present.
- The road is wet, covered in leaves, gravel or sand, or has potholes.
- It is dark, or the sun is low and in your eyes.
- Traffic is heavy, or cars ahead are slowing.
- You are coming to a curve, a hill crest, a narrow bridge or a work zone.
- Children, people walking or people on bikes are near the road.

> **Test trap:** A question may ask, "What is the safe speed on this road in heavy fog?" and offer the posted limit as a choice. The posted limit is almost never the answer when the question describes bad conditions. The safe speed is one that lets you stop within the distance you can see.

## 6.2 Posted Speed Limits

A **posted speed limit** is the number on a white Speed Limit sign. It is the highest legal speed for that stretch of road in good conditions. Limits are set from engineering studies of the road, the crash history and the area around it.

When no sign is posted, state law sets a default limit for each type of road: lower in neighborhoods and business districts, higher on rural highways and freeways. ### Special Speed Zones

Some limits apply only at certain times or places:

- **School zones:** a lower limit when children are present, when lights flash or during posted hours. Chapter 2 covers the signs.
- **Work zones:** a lower limit through road work, often with higher fines. Chapter 2 covers these signs too.
- **Variable speed limits:** some highways use electronic signs that change the limit with weather or traffic. When the electronic sign reads SPEED LIMIT, the number shown is the legal limit at that moment.
- **Advisory speeds:** a yellow plaque under a warning sign gives a safe speed for a curve or ramp. It is advice, not law, but going faster raises your risk of losing control.

### Speeding and the Ticket

Driving even a few miles per hour over the limit is speeding. Fines usually grow with how far over you were, and states add points to your record. New drivers with a learner's permit or a provisional license often face tougher penalties, including a suspension, for a single serious ticket.

> **Remember it:** The limit is the most you may drive, never the speed you must drive.

## 6.3 Driving Too Slowly

Going too slow can be as dangerous as going too fast. When you drive far below the flow of traffic, other drivers bunch up behind you, tailgate and make risky passes. Most states make it illegal to drive so slowly that you block the normal and reasonable flow of traffic, unless slower speed is needed for safety.

Some freeways post a **minimum speed** on a white sign, often under the maximum. You must not drive below it in good conditions. In bad weather, you may need to go slower than the minimum to stay safe. In that case, stay in the right lane, and leave the freeway at the next safe exit if the conditions keep you far below the flow.

If you must drive slowly, for example because you are towing a heavy trailer or climbing a steep grade, keep to the right lane. On a two-lane road, when a line of cars builds up behind you, pull into a turnout or a safe wide spot to let them pass.

> **Road tip:** The safest speed on a busy highway is usually close to the speed of the traffic around you, as long as that speed is legal. You are less likely to be hit when you are not much faster or slower than everyone else.

## 6.4 How Far It Takes to Stop

**Total stopping distance** is how far your car travels from the moment a hazard appears until the car is fully stopped. It has three parts.

| Part | What happens | What makes it longer |
|---|---|---|
| Perception distance | You see the hazard and understand it | Distraction, fatigue, alcohol, poor visibility |
| Reaction distance | Your foot moves from the gas to the brake | Distraction, fatigue, alcohol, drugs |
| Braking distance | The brakes slow the car to a stop | Speed, wet or icy road, worn tires or brakes, heavy load, downhill |

### Perception and Reaction

During perception and reaction, the car has not slowed at all. It keeps rolling at full speed while your brain notices the problem and your foot moves. For an alert driver, these two parts together take about one and a half to two and a half seconds, and longer when you are tired or distracted. That sounds small, but at 60 mph your car covers 88 feet every second. A driver who looks down at a phone for even two seconds may not start braking until the car has traveled well over 150 feet.

### Braking Distance and Speed

Braking distance does not grow evenly with speed. When you double your speed, your braking distance becomes about four times as long. A car that needs a short distance to brake from 30 mph needs about four times that distance to brake from 60 mph.

![At twice the speed, the yellow perception and orange reaction parts each double, and the red striped braking part grows to about four times as long.](fig/stopping_distance.svg)

Put all three parts together and a car moving at highway speed on dry pavement needs roughly the length of a football field to stop. On a wet road, it needs more. On ice, it can need several times as much.

> **Remember it:** Twice the speed, four times the braking distance. Small increases in speed take away a lot of your stopping room.

### Seeing Far Enough to Stop

The basic speed law and stopping distance meet here: you should be able to stop within the distance you can clearly see ahead. At night, that means within the reach of your headlights. In fog or around a blind curve, it means a much lower speed than usual. If something could be sitting just past the point where your view ends, your speed must let you stop before you reach it.

## 6.5 Following Distance

**Following distance** is the gap between your car and the vehicle ahead. Measure it in seconds, not in car lengths. A time gap grows by itself as you go faster, which is exactly what you need, because stopping distance grows too.

### How to Count It

1. Pick a fixed point ahead: a sign, a tree, a shadow or a line on the road.
2. When the rear bumper of the vehicle ahead passes that point, start counting: "one thousand one, one thousand two, one thousand three."
3. Stop counting when the front of your car reaches the same point.

The number you reach is your following distance in seconds. If you reach the point before you finish the count your state requires, you are too close. Ease off the gas and let the gap open.

### When to Leave More Room

The normal gap is for a dry road and a clear view. Add more time when:

- The road is wet, icy, snowy, or covered with gravel or loose dirt.
- It is dark, foggy or raining hard.
- You follow a large truck, a bus or a vehicle towing a trailer. They block your view of the road ahead, and some must stop often.
- You follow a motorcycle. A motorcycle can stop faster than a car, and a rider can fall.
- You follow a school bus, a vehicle carrying hazardous materials, or any vehicle that must stop at railroad crossings.
- Someone is tailgating you. Extra room ahead lets you slow down gently, so the driver behind is less likely to hit you.
- You are going down a long hill, where your brakes have to work harder.
- You are new to driving or to the car. More time helps while your skills are still growing.

### When a Car Cuts In

When another driver pulls into the gap you left, do not speed up or stay close to make a point. Ease off and build the gap back. Leaving space is not a contest, and it protects you.

> **Test trap:** Car lengths sound easy, but most test questions use seconds. If an answer says "one car length for every 10 mph" and another gives a count in seconds, the seconds answer matches what most state manuals teach.

## 6.6 Your Space Cushion

A **space cushion** is open room on every side of your car: ahead, behind, left and right. It gives you time to see trouble and a place to go if you need to swerve.

### Ahead

Following distance is the front part of your cushion. It is the part you control best, so keep it full.

### Behind

You cannot control the driver behind you, but you can help. Signal early, slow down gradually, and keep a steady speed. If someone is following too closely:

- Do not slam on your brakes to warn them. That can cause the very crash you want to avoid.
- Move to the right lane if there is one, and let them pass.
- If you cannot change lanes, slow down a little, only when the road ahead is clear, to encourage them to pass.
- Add room in front of you so you will not have to stop suddenly.

### To the Sides

Side space gives you an escape path. On a road with several lanes, avoid driving right beside another vehicle for long. Either move ahead of it or drop back. Leave room between your car and parked cars, because a door can swing open or a car can pull out without warning.

When there are hazards on both sides, such as parked cars on your right and oncoming traffic on your left, steer a middle path between them. When one hazard is worse than the other, give the worse one more room. A child on a bike near the curb needs more of your space than a parked car does.

> **Road tip:** Always know where you would go if the car ahead stopped dead. If the answer is "nowhere," open up more space now.

## 6.7 Scanning: Look Far, Look Wide

Good drivers find problems early, while there is still time to adjust. That takes **scanning**: moving your eyes in a regular pattern instead of staring at the bumper in front of you.

### Look Ahead

Aim your eyes well down the road, about 10 to 15 seconds ahead of your car. In the city that is about one block. On the highway it is about a quarter mile. Looking that far ahead lets you spot brake lights, a car waiting to pull out or a lane that ends before you are on top of it.

### Look Around

- Glance to the sides for cars, people, bikes and animals that could enter the road.
- Check your mirrors every few seconds, and always before you slow down, stop, turn or change lanes.
- At intersections, look left, then right, then left again before you go. Look left last because cars from the left reach your path first.
- Keep your eyes moving. Staring at one spot for long makes you miss what is coming from the side.

### Look for the Escape

As you scan, ask yourself three questions. What could go wrong here? What would I do? Where would I go? Planning ahead turns a surprise into a move you already prepared.

## 6.8 Blind Spots

A **blind spot** is an area around your car that you cannot see in your mirrors. The biggest ones are behind your left and right shoulders. A whole car, a motorcycle or a person on a bike can hide there.

### Check Before You Move

Before you change lanes, merge, pull away from the curb or turn across a bike lane:

1. Check your inside mirror.
2. Check the side mirror on the side you are moving to.
3. Turn your head and glance over that shoulder, through the side window. This is called a **head check**.
4. Move only when the space is clear.

Keep the head check quick, so your eyes are off the road ahead for only a moment.

### Set Your Mirrors to Help

Adjust your side mirrors so you can barely see the side of your own car in the inside edge of each mirror. This shows more of the lane next to you. It shrinks the blind spot but does not remove it.

![The light blue areas are what your mirrors show; the orange striped areas behind each shoulder are blind spots, big enough to hide a whole car.](fig/blind_spots.svg)

### Do Not Trust Sensors Alone

Many cars have **blind spot warning** lights in the mirrors or a backup camera. These systems help, but they can miss a fast car, a motorcycle or a person on foot, and dirt or snow can block them. The law and the examiner still expect you to turn your head.

### Stay Out of Other Drivers' Blind Spots

Other drivers have blind spots too. Do not ride beside another car, just behind its back corner. When you pass, move through the other driver's blind spot steadily and do not linger there. Large trucks have much bigger blind spots than cars. Chapter 9 covers those zones.

> **Test trap:** A question may ask how to check a blind spot. Mirrors alone is the wrong answer. The correct check is a quick look over your shoulder.

## Quick Review

- The basic speed law: never drive faster than is safe for current conditions, even under the posted limit.
- The posted limit is the most you may drive in good conditions; it is not a target.
- Driving so slowly that you block normal traffic can be against the law.
- Total stopping distance is perception plus reaction plus braking distance.
- Double your speed and your braking distance becomes about four times as long.
- Measure following distance in seconds using a fixed point; add more time in bad weather, behind large vehicles and when tailgated.
- Keep a space cushion on all sides, and know your escape path.
- Scan 10 to 15 seconds ahead and check mirrors every few seconds.
- Check blind spots with a quick look over your shoulder before every lane change.
