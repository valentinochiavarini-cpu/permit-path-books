# Chapter 6: Speed and Space

Most crashes happen because a driver is going too fast for what is ahead, or is too close to the car in front, and runs out of time. Speed and space are the two things you control every second you drive.

> **On the test**
>
> - The basic speed law, curves and steep hills, and why driving too slowly can also be illegal.
> - The three parts of stopping distance and what makes each longer.
> - Following distance in seconds, and when to add more.
> - Blind spots, and what to do when someone tailgates you.

## 6.1 The Basic Speed Law

The **basic speed law** applies on every road: you may never drive faster than is reasonable and safe for the conditions at that moment. Conditions include weather, light, traffic, the road surface, curves, hills, people walking nearby and how well you can see.

A Speed Limit sign of 45 does not mean 45 is safe tonight in heavy rain. If an officer decides your speed was too fast for the conditions, you can get a ticket while your speedometer reads 38.

Slow down below the limit when:

- Rain, snow, ice, fog, smoke or dust is present.
- The road is wet, covered in leaves, gravel or sand, or has potholes.
- It is dark, or the sun is low and in your eyes.
- Traffic is heavy, or cars ahead are slowing.
- You are coming to a curve, a hill crest, a narrow bridge or a work zone.
- Children, people walking or people on bikes are near the road.

> **Test trap:** When a question describes bad conditions, the posted limit is almost never the answer. The safe speed is one that lets you stop within the distance you can see.

### Curves

Running off the road, often on a curve, is one of the most common crashes for new teen drivers. Most start with too much speed going in.

- Watch for the curve warning sign and any yellow advisory speed plaque under it.
- Slow down **before** the curve, while the car is still going straight. Braking hard in the curve can make the tires lose their grip.
- Keep to the center of your lane. Do not drift over the center line or onto the shoulder.
- Look through the curve to where you want to go, and ease back onto the gas as the road straightens.

### Steep Downgrades

Before a long, steep hill down, shift to a lower gear (L, 2 or the manual mode of an automatic) so the engine helps hold the car back. Do not ride the brakes all the way down. They can overheat and **fade**, and then they may not stop you. Brake firmly to slow, then ease off and let them cool.

## 6.2 Posted Speed Limits

A **posted speed limit** is the number on a white Speed Limit sign. It is the highest legal speed for that stretch of road in good conditions, a ceiling and not a target.

When no sign is posted, state law sets a default limit for each type of road: lower in neighborhoods and business districts, higher on rural highways and freeways. The box below gives these **statutory limits**.

### Special Speed Zones

- **School zones:** a lower limit when children are present, when lights flash or during posted hours.
- **Work zones:** a lower limit through road work, often with higher fines. Chapter 2 covers the signs for both.
- **Variable speed limits:** when an electronic sign reads SPEED LIMIT, the number shown is the legal limit at that moment.
- **Advisory speeds:** a yellow plaque under a warning sign gives a safe speed for a curve or ramp. It is advice, not law, but going faster raises your risk of losing control.

Driving even a few miles per hour over the limit is speeding. The fine grows with how far over you were, and the ticket goes on your driving record (Chapter 12). For a new driver, even one or two tickets can bring a suspension.

## 6.3 Driving Too Slowly

When you drive far below the flow of traffic, other drivers bunch up behind you, tailgate and make risky passes. It is illegal to drive so slowly that you block the normal and reasonable flow of traffic, unless slower speed is needed for safety.

Some freeways post a **minimum speed** on a white sign, often under the maximum. You must not drive below it in good conditions. In bad weather, you may need to go slower than the minimum to stay safe. Then stay in the right lane, and leave the freeway at the next safe exit if the conditions keep you far below the flow.

If you must drive slowly, keep to the right lane. On a two-lane road, when a line of cars builds up behind you, pull into a turnout or a safe wide spot to let them pass.

## 6.4 How Far It Takes to Stop

**Total stopping distance** is how far your car travels from the moment a hazard appears until the car is fully stopped. It has three parts.

| Part | What happens | What makes it longer |
|---|---|---|
| Perception distance | You see the hazard and understand it | Distraction, fatigue, alcohol, poor visibility |
| Reaction distance | Your foot moves from the gas to the brake | Distraction, fatigue, alcohol, drugs |
| Braking distance | The brakes slow the car to a stop | Speed, wet or icy road, worn tires or brakes, heavy load, downhill |

### Perception and Reaction

During perception and reaction, the car keeps rolling at full speed. For an alert driver, these two parts together take about one and a half to two and a half seconds, and longer when you are tired or distracted. At 60 mph your car covers 88 feet every second.

### Braking Distance and Speed

When you double your speed, your braking distance becomes about four times as long. The figure shows how all three parts grow.

![At twice the speed, the yellow perception and orange reaction parts each double, and the red striped braking part grows to about four times as long.](fig/stopping_distance.svg)

A car at highway speed on dry pavement needs roughly the length of a football field to stop. On a wet road it needs more, and on ice several times as much.

> **Remember it:** Twice the speed, four times the braking distance.

### Seeing Far Enough to Stop

You should be able to stop within the distance you can clearly see ahead. At night, that means within the reach of your headlights. In fog or around a blind curve, it means a much lower speed than usual.

## 6.5 Following Distance

**Following distance** is the gap between your car and the vehicle ahead. Measure it in seconds rather than car lengths. A time gap grows by itself as you go faster, just as stopping distance does. The figure shows how to count it.

![Start counting when the back of the car ahead passes a fixed point, and stop when your front bumper reaches it; the box below gives the count to use.](fig/following_count.svg)

1. Pick a fixed point ahead: a sign, a tree, a shadow or a line on the road.
2. When the rear bumper of the vehicle ahead passes it, count: "one thousand one, one thousand two..." and keep counting.
3. Stop counting when the front of your car reaches the same point.

If you reach the point before you finish the count in the box below, you are too close. Ease off the gas and let the gap open.

### When to Leave More Room

The normal gap is for a dry road and a clear view. Add more time when:

- The road is wet, icy, snowy, or covered with gravel or loose dirt.
- It is dark, foggy or raining hard.
- You follow a large truck, a bus or a vehicle towing a trailer. They block your view of the road ahead.
- You follow a motorcycle. A motorcycle can stop faster than a car, and a rider can fall.
- You follow a school bus, a vehicle carrying hazardous materials, or any vehicle that must stop at railroad crossings.
- Someone is tailgating you. Extra room ahead lets you slow down gently, so the driver behind is less likely to hit you.
- A driver behind you wants to pass. Room ahead lets that driver pull back in.
- You are going down a long hill.
- You are stopped behind another car on an upgrade. It may roll back as it starts, so leave room to see its rear tires.

When another driver pulls into the gap you left, ease off and build the gap back.

> **Test trap:** Some manuals also teach "one car length for every 10 mph". Know both, but count in seconds on the road: it is easier to judge and works at any speed.

## 6.6 Your Space Cushion

A **space cushion** is open room on every side of your car: ahead, behind, left and right. It gives you time to see trouble and a place to go if you need to swerve. Following distance is the front part, and the part you control best.

### Behind

If someone is tailgating you:

- Do not slam on your brakes to warn them. That can cause a rear end crash.
- Move to the right lane if there is one, and let them pass.
- If you cannot change lanes, slow down a little, only when the road ahead is clear, to encourage them to pass.
- Add room in front of you so you will not have to stop suddenly.

### To the Sides

Side space gives you an escape path. On a road with several lanes, do not drive right beside another vehicle for long, where you sit in each other's blind spots; move ahead of it or drop back. Leave room between your car and parked cars, because a door can swing open or a car can pull out.

When there are equal hazards on both sides, such as parked cars on your right and oncoming traffic on your left, steer a middle path between them. When one hazard is worse, give it more room. A child on a bike near the curb needs more of your space than a parked car does.

## 6.7 Scanning: Look Far, Look Wide

Aim your eyes about 12 to 15 seconds ahead of your car: about a block or more in the city, about a quarter mile on the highway. That lets you find problems early.

- Glance to the sides for cars, people, bikes and animals that could enter the road.
- Check your mirrors every few seconds, and always before you slow down, stop, turn or change lanes.
- At intersections, look left, then right, then left again. Look left last because cars from the left reach your path first.
- Keep your eyes moving, and always know where you would go if the car ahead stopped.

## 6.8 Blind Spots

A **blind spot** is an area around your car that you cannot see in your mirrors. The biggest ones are behind your left and right shoulders, as the figure shows. A whole car, a motorcycle or a person on a bike can hide there.

![The light blue areas are what your mirrors show; the orange striped areas behind each shoulder are blind spots, big enough to hide a whole car.](fig/blind_spots.svg)

Before you change lanes, merge, pull away from the curb or turn across a bike lane:

1. Check your inside mirror.
2. Check the side mirror on the side you are moving to.
3. Turn your head and glance over that shoulder, through the side window. This quick **head check** is the only full check.
4. Move only when the space is clear.

Adjust your side mirrors so you can barely see the side of your own car at the inside edge of each mirror. This shows more of the next lane and shrinks the blind spot, but does not remove it.

**Blind spot warning** lights help, but they can miss a fast car, a motorcycle or a person on foot, and dirt or snow can block them. You still need to turn your head.

Other drivers have blind spots too. Do not ride just behind another car's back corner. When you pass, move through the other driver's blind spot steadily and do not linger. Large trucks have much bigger blind spots; Chapter 9 covers those.

## Quick Review

- Never drive faster than is safe for conditions, even under the posted limit.
- Stopping distance is perception plus reaction plus braking; double the speed, four times the braking.
- Count following distance in seconds; add more in bad weather, behind large vehicles and when tailgated.
- Let a tailgater pass, and keep a space cushion on all sides.
- Scan 12 to 15 seconds ahead and check blind spots with a look over your shoulder.
- Slow down before a curve, not in it; on a steep downgrade, use a lower gear instead of riding the brakes.
