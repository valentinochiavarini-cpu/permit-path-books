# Chapter 4: Right-of-Way: Who Goes First

Two cars arrive at the same intersection at the same moment. Someone has to go first, and someone has to wait. The rules that decide this are called **right-of-way** rules, and they prevent more crashes than any sign or signal. Many intersections have no light at all, so these rules are often the only thing keeping traffic sorted.

On the knowledge test, right-of-way questions are a favorite because they are easy to describe in a sentence: "You and another car reach a four way stop together. Who goes?" The answers follow a short list of rules. Learn the list and these questions turn into free points.

There is one idea to hold on to through the whole chapter. The law never gives anyone the right-of-way. It only says who must **yield**. If another driver fails to yield to you, you still have to do everything you can to avoid a crash.

> **On the test**
>
> - Know who goes first at a four way stop, including when two cars arrive together.
> - Know who yields where nothing controls the intersection, and where one road ends at another.
> - Know what to do when you enter a road from a driveway, an alley or a parking lot.
> - Know when to yield to pedestrians, including a person with a white cane or a guide dog.
> - Know what to do for an emergency vehicle, a stopped school bus and a funeral procession.

## 4.1 Yielding Is the Rule, Not Winning

To **yield** means to let another road user go first. You slow down, or stop if you need to, and wait until your path is clear. Yielding is not the same as stopping: at a yield sign you may keep rolling if no one is coming. But if someone is coming, you wait.

The **right-of-way** is the privilege of going first. The law describes who must give it up in each situation. It never tells you that you may force your way through.

- If the other driver should yield but does not, let them go. Being right is no help in a crash.
- If someone waves you on when you have to yield, check for yourself before you move. They cannot see every lane and every pedestrian for you.
- Do not give up your turn in a way that confuses others. Waving people on when the rules say you go first creates hesitation, and hesitation causes crashes.

> **Remember it:** The law tells you when to yield. It never tells you when to insist.

## 4.2 Intersections with Signs or Signals

A **controlled intersection** has a signal, stop signs or yield signs. The control tells you most of what you need.

- **Traffic signal:** follow the light, as covered in Chapter 3. On a green light, you still yield to pedestrians in the crosswalk and to vehicles already in the intersection.
- **Stop sign on your road only:** stop, then yield to all cross traffic and pedestrians. Cross traffic does not have to stop for you.
- **Yield sign:** slow down and let cross traffic and pedestrians go first. Stop if you must.

### Turning Left

When you turn left, you cross the path of oncoming traffic. That is why the left turner almost always yields. On a green light without a green arrow, wait for oncoming cars that are going straight or turning right to pass, and for pedestrians to clear the crosswalk you are turning into. The same rule applies when you turn left into a driveway or a parking lot in the middle of a block: oncoming traffic goes first.

### Turning Right

When you turn right, check the crosswalk you are crossing and look over your right shoulder for bicycles or motorcycles coming up beside you. They have the right-of-way when they are going straight.

## 4.3 Four Way and All Way Stops

At an **all way stop**, every direction has a stop sign. You will see a plaque under each stop sign that reads ALL WAY (older signs may say 4-WAY). The rules are about order, not speed.

1. **First to stop, first to go.** The driver who came to a complete stop first goes first.
2. **A tie between cars at right angles:** the car to your **right** goes first. If you are the car on the right, you go.
3. **A tie between cars facing each other:** both may go if both are driving straight or both are turning right. If one is turning left, the driver going straight or turning right goes first.

Every driver must come to a full stop, even when it is their turn and no one else is around.

| Situation | Who goes first |
|---|---|
| One car stopped earlier | The car that stopped first |
| Two cars at right angles, same time | The car on the right |
| Opposite each other, one turning left | The car going straight |

![At an all way stop the first car to stop goes first; in a tie at right angles the car on the right goes; facing each other, the car going straight goes before the left turner.](fig/ch04_four_way_stop.svg)

> **Test trap:** At an all way stop, the larger vehicle, the faster vehicle and the vehicle on the bigger road do not get to go first. Only the order of arrival and the yield to the right rule count.

> **Road tip:** Real drivers do not always follow the rules perfectly. Make eye contact, keep your foot ready on the brake, and move out only when you are sure the other driver is waiting for you.

## 4.4 Intersections with No Signs or Signals

An **uncontrolled intersection** has no signal, no stop sign and no yield sign. You find them on quiet residential streets and on rural roads. Slow down as you approach, look both ways, and be ready to stop.

- The vehicle that reaches the intersection first goes first.
- If two vehicles get there together, the one approaching from the right goes first. Give way to the car on your right.
- A driver turning left yields to oncoming traffic going straight or turning right.

### T Intersections

A **T intersection** is shaped like the letter: your street stops where it meets a cross street that keeps going. If there is no sign or signal, traffic on the **through road** (the road that continues) has the right-of-way. If your street is the one that stops, you must yield to traffic from both directions before you turn.

### Entering a Road from a Driveway, Alley or Parking Lot

When you pull out of a driveway, an alley, a parking lot or a private road, you yield to everyone. Stop before the sidewalk and check for people walking, because they have the right-of-way across the driveway. Then stop again if you need to, where you can see the road, and wait for a gap in traffic from both directions. If you are turning left, wait for a gap on both sides at once.

> **Test trap:** On a question about a driveway or alley, the answer is almost always that the driver leaving the driveway yields to pedestrians on the sidewalk and to all traffic on the road.

![With no signs, yield to the car on your right; at a T intersection, the through road goes first; leaving a driveway, stop before the sidewalk and again where you can see the road.](fig/ch04_no_signs_t_driveway.svg)

## 4.5 Pedestrians

A **pedestrian** is anyone on foot, and the term also covers a person using a wheelchair. Pedestrians have no metal around them, so the rules put them first in most situations.

- **Crosswalks:** yield to pedestrians in a marked crosswalk and in the unmarked crosswalk that exists at most intersections. If someone has started across, stop and wait until they are out of your path.
- **Turning:** before any turn, check the crosswalk you are turning into. People crossing with the WALK signal often step out at the same time you start your turn.
- **Stopped cars at a crosswalk:** if a car in the next lane has stopped at a crosswalk, assume someone is crossing in front of it. Stop too, and never pass it.
- **Sidewalks:** when you cross a sidewalk to enter or leave a driveway, people on the sidewalk go first.

A pedestrian who crosses in the middle of a block, away from a crosswalk, is supposed to yield to cars. But you still must take every step to avoid hitting them. Slow down and stop if you have to.

### Blind Pedestrians

A person carrying a **white cane** or walking with a **guide dog** is usually blind or has low vision. Every state requires drivers to yield to them. Stop and wait until they are well clear of your path. Do not honk or rev your engine, which can confuse them, and do not call out instructions. Electric and hybrid cars are very quiet at low speed, so be extra patient when you are driving one. If the person pulls the cane back and steps away from the curb, they are usually telling you to go ahead.

## 4.6 Emergency Vehicles

An **emergency vehicle** is a police car, fire truck, ambulance or other authorized vehicle using a siren, flashing lights or both. Usually the flashing lights are red, blue or red and white. When one is coming toward you or from behind you, from any direction:

1. Pull over to the **right edge** of the road, as close as you safely can.
2. **Stop** and stay stopped until it has passed.
3. Watch for a second emergency vehicle before you pull back out. They often travel in pairs.

If you are in an intersection, drive through it first, then pull over. Do not stop inside the intersection, where you block the emergency vehicle's path. On a one way street, pull to the right edge unless your state lets you use the nearest edge, as some states do. If you hear a siren but cannot tell where it is coming from, slow down and pull over until you are sure.

You must stop for an emergency vehicle that is on the other side of a two way road too, unless a median or barrier separates you. Never follow an emergency vehicle closely to get through traffic.

### Move Over

Every state has a **Move Over law**. It protects people working beside the road. When you approach an emergency vehicle that is stopped on or next to the road with its lights flashing, you must:

- **Move over** out of the lane next to it, if you can do so safely, or
- **Slow down** well below the speed limit if you cannot move over.

Many states extend this rule to tow trucks, road maintenance and utility vehicles, and in some states to any vehicle that is stopped with its hazard lights on. The lights tell you someone may be outside the vehicle a few feet from your lane.

## 4.7 School Buses

A school bus uses two sets of flashing lights to warn you. They work like a yellow light and then a red light.

- **Flashing yellow lights** on the front and back mean the bus is about to stop to load or unload children. Slow down and get ready to stop.
- **Flashing red lights** and a **stop arm** (a red octagon that swings out from the left side of the bus) mean the bus has stopped. You must stop.

Passing a school bus that is stopped with red lights flashing and the stop arm out is illegal in every state. If you are behind the bus, stop a safe distance back. If you are coming toward it on a two lane road, stop before you reach it. Stay stopped until the red lights go off, the stop arm folds in and the bus starts to move, or until a police officer, or in some states the bus driver, signals you to go.

Children are hardest to see near the bus. They may run across the road in front of it or bend down to pick up something they dropped. When you start again, go slowly and watch the roadside.

On wider roads and on divided highways, whether traffic on the other side must stop depends on your state, and so does the minimum stopping distance. Your state box below gives them.

## 4.8 Transit Buses, Funeral Processions and Others

### Transit Buses

City buses stop often to pick up passengers. When a bus signals to pull back into traffic from a stop, let it in when you safely can. Some states require you to yield to a transit bus that is signaling to re-enter traffic. Watch for passengers who step out from in front of a stopped bus.

### Funeral Processions

A **funeral procession** is a group of cars traveling together behind a hearse to a cemetery. The cars usually drive with headlights on, and often with small flags or hazard lights. Many processions have a police or funeral escort at the front. Many states let a properly led procession continue through an intersection together once the lead vehicle has entered legally, even if the light changes. Do not cut into the line, drive through it or join it to get through a light. Wait for it to pass, then go.

### Others Who Get Special Treatment

- **Flaggers and crossing guards:** obey them over signs and signals, as covered in Chapter 2.
- **Trains:** a train always has the right-of-way at a crossing. It cannot stop or swerve. Chapter 5 covers railroad crossings.
- **Vehicles already in a roundabout:** you yield to them when you enter. Chapter 5 explains roundabouts.

## Quick Review

- The law says who must yield; it never gives anyone the right to force their way through.
- At an all way stop, the first to stop goes first; at the same time, yield to the car on the right.
- A driver turning left yields to oncoming traffic going straight or turning right.
- At a T intersection with no signs, traffic on the through road goes first.
- Leaving a driveway or alley, yield to pedestrians on the sidewalk and to all road traffic.
- Always yield to a person with a white cane or a guide dog.
- For an emergency vehicle with a siren or flashing lights, pull to the right edge and stop; clear an intersection first.
- Move over a lane, or slow down, for vehicles stopped beside the road with lights flashing.
- A school bus showing red lights and its stop arm means you stop and stay stopped until the lights go off, the arm folds in and the bus moves.
- Do not cut into or drive through a funeral procession.
