# Chapter 5: Turns, Roundabouts and Railroad Crossings

Turning looks simple from the passenger seat. From the driver's seat it is a stack of small jobs done in the right order: check, signal, slow, position, look again, steer, and pick the correct lane on the far side. Most crashes at intersections happen while someone is turning, usually because one of those jobs got skipped.

The knowledge test checks that you know the order and the lane positions. Expect questions on which lane to turn from and into, what your arm signals mean, which way traffic flows in a roundabout, and how far to stop from a railroad track. Examiners on the road test watch your turns more closely than almost anything else you do.

Railroad crossings get their own section at the end. They do not come up often in daily driving, but when they do, a mistake is rarely small.

> **On the test**
>
> - Put the steps of a turn in the right order and know when to signal.
> - Know which lane to start in and end in for right and left turns, including on one way streets.
> - Know where U-turns are not allowed and how to make a three-point turn.
> - Know how to enter, drive and leave a roundabout, including the multi-lane kind.
> - Know the three arm signals.
> - Know where to stop at a railroad crossing and what to do if your car stalls on the tracks.

## 5.1 Every Turn, Step by Step

Use the same routine for every turn, so it becomes automatic.

1. **Plan early.** Know where you are turning before you get there. Move into the correct lane well ahead of the intersection, checking mirrors and your blind spot first.
2. **Signal.** Turn on your signal before you start slowing down, so drivers behind you know why you are braking. In town, that usually means about half a block ahead. Do not signal so early that drivers think you are turning into an earlier driveway.
3. **Slow down** before the turn, while your wheels are still straight. Braking in the middle of a turn can upset the car.
4. **Look** left, right, ahead and left again. Check the crosswalk you are about to cross and the one on the far side.
5. **Turn** smoothly at a low speed, looking where you want the car to go, not at the curb.
6. **Finish in the correct lane**, let the wheel slide back to center, and turn off your signal if it does not cancel on its own. Then speed up to match traffic.

> **Road tip:** Look through the turn. Your hands follow your eyes. If you stare at a parked car on the corner, you will drift toward it. Look at the lane you want to end in.

## 5.2 Right Turns

For a **right turn**, start from the rightmost lane, or from the right turn lane if there is one. Stay close to the curb as you turn, without riding over it. Finish in the right lane of the new road.

Avoid **swinging wide**, which means drifting left before the turn or across lanes after it. A wide swing invites a car or a bicycle to slip into the gap on your right, and it can put you in the path of oncoming traffic.

Before you start, look over your right shoulder for bicycles, scooters and motorcycles coming up beside you. Then check the crosswalk. People crossing with the walk signal have the right-of-way.

### Turning Across a Bike Lane

Many streets have a **bike lane** along the right edge, marked by a solid white line and a bicycle symbol. When you turn right across one, the people riding in it are going straight, and they have the right-of-way. Signal early, check your mirror and look over your shoulder, and let any cyclist pass before you turn.

Where the bike lane line changes from solid to broken near the corner, many states let you, or even require you to, move into the bike lane just before your turn, after yielding to cyclists. Where the line stays solid, turn from your own lane. Never use the bike lane to pass, and never cut across a rider. Chapter 9 covers sharing the road with bicycles.

## 5.3 Left Turns

For a **left turn**, start from the lane next to the center line, or from the left turn lane if there is one. Turn into the lane just right of the center line on the new road, which is usually the left lane on your side.

Do not cut the corner. Follow the path a car would take if it were drawn as a gentle curve through the intersection, so you stay out of the lane of cars waiting to turn left from the other road.

### Waiting to Turn Left

When your light is green but you do not have a green arrow, your left turn is **unprotected**: oncoming cars may keep coming. Pull forward to the waiting point only if no one ahead of you is turning left. Keep your wheels pointed straight while you wait. If a car hits you from behind while your wheels are turned, you will be pushed into oncoming traffic.

Turn when oncoming traffic clears or stops for a red light. Do not turn just because an oncoming driver has a right turn signal on. Wait until you can see that the car is really turning.

> **Test trap:** The question may ask what position your wheels should be in while you wait to turn left. The answer is straight ahead, not turned to the left.

## 5.4 Turns on One Way Streets

On a **one way street**, all lanes move in the same direction, and the rules about where to start a turn move with them.

| Turn | Start in | End in |
|---|---|---|
| Two way to one way, left | Lane nearest the center | Left lane of the one way street |
| One way to two way, left | Far left lane | Lane just right of the center line |
| One way to one way, left | Far left lane | Left lane |
| One way to one way, right | Far right lane | Right lane |

The idea is simple: a left turn starts from the left side of the road you are on, and a right turn starts from the right. On a one way street, the left side is the far left lane, next to the left curb.

## 5.5 Multiple Turn Lanes

At busy intersections you may see two or even three lanes that all turn the same way. Each lane has its own path through the turn. Stay in your lane from start to finish: the left lane turns into the left lane, the next lane into the next lane. White arrows on the pavement and lane signs show which lanes may turn and which may also go straight. Dotted lines painted through the intersection often trace the path for you.

Never change lanes in the middle of a multi-lane turn. Cars beside you are turning at the same moment, and there is no room to move over.

> **Remember it:** Pick your lane before the turn. Stay in it through the turn. Change lanes only after the turn is done, with a signal.

## 5.6 U-Turns and Three-Point Turns

### U-Turns

A **U-turn** is a turn in one smooth curve to go back the way you came. Make it only where it is legal and safe, starting from the far left lane or the left turn lane. Signal left, yield to oncoming traffic and to anyone in the crosswalk, and turn only when you have a large gap.

The places where U-turns are almost never allowed are the places where other drivers cannot see you or do not expect you:

- where a NO U-TURN sign is posted
- on or near a curve or the top of a hill, where drivers cannot see you in time
- on a freeway or other limited access highway
- at or on a railroad crossing
- anywhere it would block or endanger traffic

States add their own rules, for example about U-turns in business districts or at intersections with signals.

### Three-Point Turns

A **three-point turn** (sometimes called a **K turn**) turns your car around on a narrow street when there is no driveway or side road to use. Many road tests include it. Choose a spot with good sight lines in both directions, away from hills and curves.

1. Signal right and pull over to the right edge. Stop. Then signal left and check all directions, including your blind spot.
2. Turn the wheel sharply left and move forward slowly across the road. Stop before your front wheels touch the far curb or edge.
3. Check traffic again. Shift to reverse, turn the wheel sharply right, look over your shoulder and back up slowly. Stop before your rear wheels reach the curb or edge you started from.
4. Shift to drive, check traffic, turn the wheel left and pull forward into the lane going the other way.

If the street is very narrow, you may need more than three moves. That is fine on the road test as long as each one is controlled and you keep checking for traffic.

## 5.7 Roundabouts

A **roundabout** is an intersection built as a ring: every car travels around a raised island in the middle and peels off at its exit. There are no signals and no left turns across traffic, so the crashes that do happen are usually slow, glancing ones. You will meet more roundabouts every year.

### How to Drive One

1. **Slow down** as you approach. Read the signs to know which exit you need and which lane to use.
2. **Yield** to pedestrians in the crosswalk before the roundabout, then stop at the yield line if needed.
3. **Look left.** Traffic already in the roundabout comes from your left and has the right-of-way. Wait for a safe gap.
4. **Enter to the right** and travel **counterclockwise** around the island.
5. **Keep moving.** Do not stop inside the roundabout unless you must to avoid a crash. Do not pass anyone, and do not change lanes inside it.
6. **Signal right** just before your exit, and yield to pedestrians in the crosswalk as you leave.

If you miss your exit, go around again. Backing up or stopping in the circle is far more dangerous than taking an extra lap.

### Multi-Lane Roundabouts

Larger roundabouts have two lanes or more. Choose your lane before you enter, just as you would at a normal intersection.

| Where you are going | Lane to enter |
|---|---|
| Right (first exit) | Right lane |
| Straight ahead | Either lane, as signs show |
| Left or back the way you came | Left lane |

Stay in your lane all the way around, and leave from the same lane you entered unless signs tell you otherwise. Watch for drivers next to you who are leaving at the next exit: they will cross in front of the outside lane.

### Large Vehicles and Emergency Vehicles

Trucks and buses need extra room in a roundabout. Many roundabouts have a raised **truck apron**, a slightly raised strip around the island that large vehicles can roll onto with their back wheels. Do not drive beside a large truck in a roundabout. It may need both lanes.

If an emergency vehicle comes up while you are in a roundabout, do not stop inside. Keep going to your exit, leave the roundabout, then pull over to the right. If you have not entered yet, pull to the right and let the emergency vehicle go through first.

> **Test trap:** In a roundabout, traffic moves counterclockwise, which means you turn right to enter and keep the island on your left. Any answer that has you entering to the left, or yielding to cars that are still waiting to enter, is wrong.

## 5.8 Hand Signals

Your turn signals can fail, and bright sun can make them hard to see. You also need to know arm signals to read cyclists. Every state uses the same three, given with your left arm out the driver's window.

| Arm position | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Straight out to the left | Left turn |
| Out and bent up at the elbow | Right turn |
| Out and bent down at the elbow | Slow down or stop |

Many cyclists signal a right turn by holding the right arm straight out to the right. Treat that as a right turn signal too.

## 5.9 Railroad Crossings

A train cannot swerve, and it takes a very long distance to stop. A freight train going 55 mph can need a mile or more, even after the engineer hits the emergency brake. At a crossing, the train always goes first.

### What You Will See

- **Advance warning:** a round yellow sign with a black X and RR, and often a large X with RR painted on your lane. Slow down and get ready to stop.
- **Crossbuck:** a white X-shaped sign at the tracks. It works like a yield sign. A plaque below may show the number of tracks.
- **Flashing red lights:** two red lights that flash one after the other on a post next to the crossbuck. They mean a train is coming. Stop.
- **Gates:** red and white striped arms that lower across the road. Stop and stay stopped until the gates are fully up and the lights have stopped flashing.
- **Stop line:** a white line on your lane, at least 15 feet from the nearest rail.

### Where to Stop

When you must stop for a train, the painted line is your stopping point. Without one, stop no closer than 15 feet from the nearest rail and no more than 50 feet away, the distance most states use. That gap keeps you clear of the train, which hangs out past the rails by about three feet on each side.

### Crossing Safely

- Slow down, look both ways and listen. Turn down the radio and open a window if visibility or noise makes it hard to hear.
- Never drive around lowered gates, and never start across while lights are flashing.
- Never race a train to the crossing. Trains are larger and faster than they look.
- After one train passes on a crossing with more than one track, wait. A second train may be coming on another track, hidden behind the first.
- Do not stop on the tracks. Before you start across, make sure there is room on the far side for your whole car. If traffic is backed up past the tracks, wait behind the stop line until it moves.
- Do not shift gears while on the tracks. A stalled engine in the middle of a crossing is the worst place to be.

> **Remember it:** The train is always closer and faster than it looks. If lights flash or gates move, stop and stay stopped.

### If Your Car Stalls on the Tracks

1. **Get everyone out right away**, even if no train is in sight. Leave belongings behind.
2. **Move away from the tracks.** If a train is coming, walk or run toward the train but away from the tracks at an angle, about 45 degrees. When a train hits a car, wreckage is thrown ahead, the same way the train is heading. Moving toward the train keeps you out of that path.
3. **Call the number on the blue sign.** Public railroad crossings have a blue **Emergency Notification System** sign, usually on the crossbuck post or the signal post. It shows a phone number and a crossing ID number. Call it to tell the railroad about the stalled car so it can warn trains. If there is no sign, call 911.

Drivers of school buses, trucks carrying hazardous materials and some other vehicles must stop at every crossing, even when no train is coming. If you are behind one, expect it to stop and be ready to stop too.

## Quick Review

- Signal before you slow down for a turn, and slow down before you start steering.
- Turn right from the right lane into the right lane, and left from the lane nearest the center into the lane just right of the center line.
- Keep your wheels straight while you wait to turn left.
- In a multi-lane turn, stay in your lane from start to finish.
- Do not make a U-turn where drivers cannot see you, on a freeway or at a railroad crossing.
- In a roundabout, yield to traffic already circling, enter to the right and travel counterclockwise.
- Arm straight out means left, bent up means right, bent down means slow or stop.
- With a train coming and no stop line, wait at least 15 feet and not more than 50 feet back from the closest rail.
- Never stop on the tracks; only start across when there is room on the other side.
- If you stall on the tracks, get out, move toward the train at an angle, away from the tracks, and call the number on the blue sign.
