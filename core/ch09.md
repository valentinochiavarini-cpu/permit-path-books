# Chapter 9: Sharing the Road

Your car is not the only thing on the road. In one short trip you may pass a jogger, a cyclist, a motorcycle, a delivery truck, a city bus, a tractor and a deer. Each one moves differently, sees differently and stops differently. Driving well means knowing how each of them behaves and leaving room for it.

The people outside a car have almost no protection. A crash that bends your bumper can kill a person on foot or on a bike. That is why the law puts most of the duty of care on the driver of the larger, faster vehicle, and that is usually you.

On the knowledge test, this topic shows up as short situation questions: a cyclist ahead on a narrow road, a truck signaling a right turn, a person with a white cane at a corner. The right answer is almost always the one that gives the other road user more time and more space.

> **On the test**
>
> - When you must yield to pedestrians, including at unmarked crosswalks and to blind pedestrians.
> - How to pass a cyclist safely and how to turn across a bike lane.
> - Why a motorcycle gets a full lane and why it is hard to judge its speed.
> - Where a large truck's blind spots (no-zones) are and why trucks swing wide on turns.
> - What the slow moving vehicle emblem means and how to pass farm equipment or a horse and buggy.
> - What to do when an animal is in the road.

## 9.1 Pedestrians

A **pedestrian** is anyone on foot, and that includes a person in a wheelchair or using a mobility scooter. Pedestrians are hard to see, especially at dusk, at night and in rain, and they often assume you see them when you do not.

### Crosswalks You Can See and Ones You Cannot

A **marked crosswalk** has white painted lines across the road. An **unmarked crosswalk** has no paint at all: it is the place where the sidewalk would continue across the street at an intersection. In every state, you must yield to a pedestrian who is crossing in a crosswalk, marked or unmarked.

- Stop before the crosswalk, not on it. Leave the whole crossing clear.
- Stop far enough back that drivers in the next lane can see the person too.
- Wait until the pedestrian has crossed your half of the road, or is safely past your path, before you move.

> **Test trap:** A question may describe an intersection with no painted lines and a person waiting to cross. Do not pick "the pedestrian must wait because there is no crosswalk." The corner of an intersection is an unmarked crosswalk, and you must yield.

### Never Pass a Car Stopped at a Crosswalk

You are in the right lane. The car in the left lane stops for no clear reason in front of a crosswalk. Assume someone is crossing in front of it. The stopped car hides that person from you, and you hide your car from them. Slow down and stop too. Passing a vehicle stopped for a pedestrian at a crosswalk is illegal in most states and is one of the most common ways pedestrians are hit.

### Turning and Backing

Pedestrians are most at risk when you turn. When the light turns green and you turn right or left, people crossing the street you are turning into also have a green or a WALK signal. Look into the crosswalk on the street you are entering before you start the turn, and yield.

When you back out of a driveway or a parking space, children can be directly behind you and below the line of your rear window. Walk around the car before you get in if children are nearby, then back slowly and keep looking.

### Blind and Low Vision Pedestrians

A person carrying a **white cane** (often with a red tip) or walking with a **guide dog** may be blind or have low vision. Every state has a white cane law that requires drivers to yield to them.

- Stop and stay stopped until the person has crossed. Give them the right of way even if your light is green.
- Do not honk to signal that you are waiting. The person listens to traffic sounds to decide when to cross, and a horn can confuse or startle them.
- Do not pull into the crosswalk or stop where your car blocks their path.
- If the person pulls the cane back and steps away from the curb, that usually means they want you to go on.

### Children, Older Adults and People Who Are Distracted

Children are small, move fast and do not judge speed well. Near schools, parks and homes, expect a child to run out after a ball or a pet. Older adults may cross slowly and may not finish in one signal. A person looking at a phone or wearing earbuds may step out without looking. In each case, slow down early and be ready to stop.

> **Road tip:** At night a person in dark clothes may be invisible until your low beams are almost on them. On unlit roads, use your high beams when no one is coming toward you, and scan the shoulders, not just your lane.

## 9.2 Bicycles

In every state, a person riding a bicycle on the road has the same rights and duties as the driver of a vehicle. A cyclist must obey signals and signs, ride in the same direction as traffic, and signal turns. You must treat a cyclist as a slow vehicle, not as an obstacle.

### Where Cyclists Ride

Cyclists usually keep to the right side of the lane, but they may move left to avoid parked cars, debris, potholes or a lane that is too narrow to share, and to set up for a left turn. A cyclist riding near the center of a narrow lane is doing that on purpose, to stop drivers from squeezing past.

A **bike lane** is a lane marked for cyclists, usually with a solid white line and a bicycle symbol on the pavement. Do not drive or park in a bike lane. You may cross it to reach a driveway or parking space, and many states let or require you to enter it just before a right turn, after you yield to any cyclist in it. Some bike lanes are painted green where they cross an intersection or a driveway, to show where cars and bikes meet.

A **sharrow** is a bicycle symbol with two arrowhead shapes above it, painted in a shared lane. It tells drivers to expect cyclists in the lane and tells cyclists where to ride.

### Passing a Cyclist

Pass a cyclist the same way you pass a slow car: only when you can see far enough ahead and when you can give plenty of room.

- Slow down as you approach.
- Leave a wide gap. Most states set a minimum of at least 3 feet, and some require more or require you to change lanes when you can.
- If oncoming traffic or a curve does not leave room, wait behind the cyclist until it does.
- After you pass, do not cut back in sharply or slow down right in front of the rider.

### Turning Across a Cyclist

The **right hook** is a crash in which a driver passes a cyclist and then turns right across the cyclist's path. Before a right turn, check your right mirror and look over your right shoulder for a bike coming up beside you. If a cyclist is close, let them go through first, then turn behind them.

When you turn left, an oncoming cyclist is moving faster than most people expect. Judge the gap as you would for an oncoming car, and if you are not sure, wait.

### Opening Your Door

A cyclist riding past parked cars can be thrown into traffic by a car door that swings open. Before you open your door on the street side, check your side mirror and look back over your shoulder. A good habit is to open the door with the hand farther from it. That turns your body toward the road and makes the look back automatic.

> **Remember it:** Before you turn right, open a door or pull away from the curb, ask yourself one question: "Is there a bike beside me?"

## 9.3 Motorcycles

A **motorcycle** has the same right to a full lane as a car. Most crashes between a car and a motorcycle happen because the driver of the car did not see the rider, often while turning left in front of an oncoming motorcycle.

### Why Motorcycles Are Hard to See and Judge

A motorcycle is narrow, so it can hide behind a pillar of your windshield, in a mirror blind spot, or behind another car. Because it is small, it looks farther away and slower than it really is. When you see a motorcycle coming toward you and you want to turn left, assume it is closer and faster than it looks.

### Rules for Sharing a Lane

- Never share a lane with a motorcycle. Do not drive beside one in the same lane, even if the rider stays near one edge.
- Pass a motorcycle as you would a car: move fully into the next lane, then return only when you can see the whole motorcycle in your rearview mirror.
- Follow a motorcycle at a greater distance than you would a car. A motorcycle can stop quickly, and a rider may slow by easing off the throttle or downshifting, which does not turn on the brake light.
- A rider may move from one side of the lane to the other to avoid gravel, potholes, wind from trucks or wet paint. Expect it.

### Turn Signals and Weather

Many motorcycle turn signals do not shut off by themselves. Before you pull out in front of a motorcycle that is signaling, wait until you are sure it is really turning. Rain, wind and slick surfaces like metal grates, railroad tracks and painted lines affect a motorcycle far more than a car, so give riders extra room in bad weather.

> **Test trap:** "Since a motorcycle is smaller, a car may share its lane if there is enough room." That is false in every state. A motorcycle is entitled to the full width of its lane.

## 9.4 Large Trucks

A loaded tractor trailer can weigh 20 to 30 times as much as a car. When the two collide, the people in the car take almost all the harm. Most of these crashes are avoidable if you know three things about trucks: where the driver cannot see, how far the truck needs to stop, and how it turns.

### The No-Zones

A **no-zone** is any area around a truck or bus where the driver cannot see you. Trucks have four large ones:

- **In front:** the area just ahead of the cab. A truck driver sits high and cannot see a car that cuts in close.
- **Behind:** a long area straight behind the trailer. There is no rearview mirror view through the trailer.
- **Left side:** an area alongside the cab and under the driver's side mirror.
- **Right side:** a much larger area along the passenger side, which can cover two or more lanes and run most of the length of the trailer. This is the most dangerous no-zone.

Here is a quick check: **look for the driver's face in the truck's big side mirror. No face in the mirror means you are invisible to the driver.** Do not linger beside a truck. Pass it steadily or drop back.

### Following a Truck

Stay well back from a truck. If you follow too closely, you are in its rear no-zone, the trailer blocks your view of everything ahead, and if the truck stops short you may slide under the back of the trailer. When you are stopped behind a truck on a hill, leave extra room, because it may roll back slightly when it starts.

### Passing a Truck and Merging in Front

- Pass on the left, where the driver can see you better. Avoid passing on the right.
- Pass steadily. Do not sit beside the trailer.
- Before you move back in front, wait until you can see the whole front of the truck in your rearview mirror. Then signal and move over without slowing down.
- Never cut in close and then brake. A truck at highway speed can need a distance close to the length of two football fields to stop.
- Do not pass a truck on a long downhill grade. Trucks pick up speed going down, and the gap can close fast.

### Wide Turns

A truck's rear wheels follow a shorter path than its front wheels, so the driver must swing wide to make a turn. To turn right, a truck often moves to the left first, or starts the turn from the second lane. It may look as if the truck is turning left.

If you see a truck signaling a right turn, stay back. Never try to squeeze into the space between the truck and the curb on its right. That gap closes as the trailer swings around, and a car caught there can be crushed.

> **Remember it:** Trucks need more of everything: more space to stop, more room to turn, more time to speed up. Give it to them.

### Wind and Spray

A large truck pushes a wall of air. When it passes you, or you pass it, your car may be pushed sideways or pulled toward the truck. Keep both hands on the wheel. In rain, trucks throw up spray that can blind you for a second or two, so turn your wipers up before you pass.

## 9.5 Buses

**Transit buses** stop often and pull in and out of traffic. Give a bus room when it is stopped, and watch for passengers walking in front of it or running to catch it. When a bus signals that it is pulling back into traffic, let it in. Some states require you to yield to a transit bus re-entering traffic, and in every state it is the safe choice.

Buses have no-zones like trucks and make wide turns like trucks. The same rules apply: stay out of the blind spots and never squeeze between a turning bus and the curb.

**School buses** have their own strict stopping law, with flashing red lights and a stop arm. Chapter 4 covers when you must stop for a school bus and when you may keep going.

## 9.6 Slow Vehicles: Farm Equipment and Horse-Drawn Buggies

On rural roads you may come up behind a tractor, a combine, a road maintenance vehicle or a horse-drawn buggy. These vehicles may travel at 25 mph or less, and some much slower.

### The Slow Moving Vehicle Emblem

The **slow moving vehicle emblem** is a fluorescent orange triangle with a red reflective border, pointing up, mounted on the back of the vehicle. It means the vehicle is built to go no faster than about 25 mph. When you see it, slow down at once. At highway speed you close the gap very fast: a car at 55 mph gains about 44 feet every second on a tractor going 25.

### Passing Slow Vehicles

- Be patient. Wait for a stretch where passing is legal and you can see far enough ahead.
- Farm equipment can be wider than its lane. It may move onto the shoulder to let you by, but the shoulder may not hold its weight, so do not count on it.
- Watch for signs that a tractor will turn left into a field or a driveway. Do not pass it if it is slowing near a gate or farm entrance. Farm vehicles may have no turn signals, or ones you cannot see.

### Horses and Riders

A horse can spook at a sudden noise and jump into the road. When you come up on a horse and rider or a horse-drawn buggy:

- Slow down well before you reach it.
- Do not honk, rev your engine or race past.
- Pass slowly with plenty of room, and pull back in only when you are well ahead.
- A buggy at night may show only small lamps and reflectors, so scan the shoulders on rural roads.

## 9.7 Animals in the Road

Deer, elk, moose and farm animals cause many crashes, most often at dawn, at dusk and at night, and most often in fall. Yellow diamond warning signs with an animal symbol mark places where animals cross often.

- Where animals cross, slow down and scan both shoulders. At night, use high beams when no one is coming toward you. An animal's eyes often shine in your headlights before you see its body.
- Deer usually move in groups. If one crosses, expect more to follow.
- If an animal is in your path, brake firmly and stay in your lane. Do not swerve. Swerving can send you into oncoming traffic, a tree or a ditch, and those crashes are often worse than hitting the animal.
- A honk or a flash of your lights may move an animal off the road. Do not count on it.
- If you do hit an animal, pull off the road if you can, turn on your hazard flashers and do not walk up to the animal. An injured animal can kick or bite. Chapter 10 covers what to do after a crash.

> **Test trap:** Many people answer "swerve to avoid the deer." The expected answer is to brake firmly and stay in your lane. Steering off the road to miss an animal is a common cause of serious crashes.

## 9.8 People Working on the Road

Road workers stand a few feet from moving traffic all day. Flaggers, survey crews, tow truck operators and utility crews are all at risk from drivers who do not slow down.

- Slow down at the first orange sign and keep your speed down until you pass the END ROAD WORK sign.
- Obey a flagger's STOP and SLOW paddle, and any hand signal from a flagger, over other signs and signals in the zone.
- Leave extra space. Equipment may back up or pull out without warning.
- Do not change lanes inside the zone unless the signs or channeling devices tell you to.
- When you see a stopped tow truck, utility truck or road crew vehicle with flashing lights on the shoulder, move over a lane if you safely can or slow down. Chapter 4 covers the Move Over law.

## Quick Review

- Yield to pedestrians in every crosswalk, marked or unmarked.
- Never pass a vehicle that is stopped for someone at a crosswalk.
- A white cane or a guide dog means stop and wait; do not honk.
- Cyclists have the rights and duties of drivers; pass them slowly with a wide gap.
- Before turning right or opening a door, check for a bike beside you.
- A motorcycle gets the full lane; never share it.
- No driver's face in a truck's side mirror means the driver has no view of you.
- Never squeeze between a turning truck and the curb.
- An orange triangle with a red border means a slow vehicle; slow down at once.
- For a deer in your lane, brake firmly and stay in your lane.
