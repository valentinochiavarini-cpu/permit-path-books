# Chapter 7: Lanes, Passing and Highway Driving

Every lane change is a small decision with a big cost if you get it wrong. You move a two-ton car sideways into a space where someone else may already be. Passing on a two-lane road adds oncoming traffic to that decision. On a freeway, everything happens faster, and mistakes travel farther before anyone can stop.

The knowledge test asks about all of this in plain scenes: you want to pass a slow truck near a hill, you are on an on-ramp and traffic is heavy, you missed your exit, your car breaks down on the freeway. The right answers follow a few simple ideas: see the whole picture, signal early, move one lane at a time and never force your way in.

Freeways are the safest roads per mile driven, because traffic flows in one direction with no cross streets. They stay safe only when every driver merges, changes lanes and exits in a way the others can predict.

> **On the test**
>
> - Know which lane to use for normal driving, for passing and for slower traffic.
> - Know the steps for a safe lane change, including the head check.
> - Know where passing is not allowed and when you may pass on the right.
> - Know what to do when another car is passing you.
> - Know how to enter, drive on and leave a freeway, including what to do if you miss your exit.
> - Know what to do if your car breaks down on a highway.

## 7.1 Choosing a Lane

On a road with two or more lanes going your way, each lane has a job.

| Lane | Best use |
|---|---|
| Right lane | Slower traffic, entering and leaving, getting ready to turn right |
| Middle lanes | Steady through traffic |
| Left lane | Passing and getting ready to turn left |

The basic rule in most states is **keep right except to pass**. Drive in the right lane, or the lanes to the right, unless you are passing, turning left or the right lane is ending or blocked. Some states make it a ticketable offense to stay in the left lane when you are not passing. Even where it is not, sitting in the left lane holds up faster traffic and leads to passing on the right, which is riskier for everyone.

On a freeway with three or more lanes, the middle lanes are often the smoothest choice for a long trip. You avoid the merging traffic at every on-ramp and leave the far left lane open for passing.

Stay in one lane as much as you can. Each lane change adds risk, so do not weave from lane to lane to gain a few car lengths.

## 7.2 Changing Lanes Safely

A lane change takes only a few seconds, but the checks before it are what keep you safe. Use the same steps every time so they become a habit.

1. **Check your mirrors.** Look in the inside mirror and the side mirror on the side you want to move to. Find a gap in the traffic.
2. **Signal.** Turn on your signal early enough to warn the drivers around you. Many states require a signal at least 100 feet before you change lanes or turn; on a freeway, give several seconds of warning.
3. **Head check.** Glance over your shoulder toward the lane you are entering, through the side window, to clear your blind spot.
4. **Move smoothly.** Keep your speed steady, or speed up a little, and steer gently into the center of the new lane. Do not slow down as you change lanes on a highway.
5. **Cancel your signal.** Make sure it turns off once you are in the lane.

If something changes during the move, such as a car speeding up in the next lane, stay where you are or ease back into your own lane.

> **Road tip:** When you want to cross several lanes, move one lane at a time. Signal, check and move into the next lane, settle in, then repeat for the next one. Cutting across many lanes at once is how drivers get hit by cars they never saw.

### Watch Two Lanes Over

On a road with three or more lanes, a car two lanes away may move into the same open lane you want, at the same moment. Before you move, look at the lane beyond your target lane as well.

### Lines That Mean Do Not Cross

A broken white line between lanes means you may change lanes when it is safe. A solid white line means changing lanes is discouraged. A **double solid white line** means you may not cross it at all. You see double white lines at the edge of some special lanes and near the gore area where an exit ramp splits off. Chapter 3 covers these markings in full.

## 7.3 Passing on a Two-Lane Road

Passing on a road with one lane each way is one of the most dangerous moves in everyday driving, because for a few seconds you are in the lane of oncoming traffic. Before you pass, ask yourself whether you really need to. Passing a car that is going only a little slower than you saves very little time.

### When You May Pass

You may pass on the left only when all of these are true:

- The center line on your side is broken yellow, and no sign says passing is not allowed.
- You can see far enough ahead to finish the pass with plenty of room before any oncoming car reaches you.
- The left lane is clear well ahead and no one behind you has started to pass you.
- You can pass without going over the speed limit.

### How to Pass

1. Stay well back from the vehicle ahead so you can see down the road.
2. Check ahead for oncoming traffic, side roads and driveways.
3. Check your mirrors and blind spot, then signal left.
4. Move fully into the left lane and speed up smoothly.
5. Keep going until you can see both headlights of the car you passed in your inside mirror. Then you have enough room to return.
6. Signal right and move back into your lane. Do not slow down until you are back in your own lane.
7. Cancel your signal.

If an oncoming car appears and you cannot finish in time, brake, drop back behind the car you were passing and return to your lane. Giving up on a pass is always better than forcing it.

### Where Passing Is Not Allowed

Do not pass in any of these places, even if no sign or marking warns you:

- Where a solid yellow line is on your side of the center line.
- On a curve or near the top of a hill, where you cannot see far enough ahead.
- In or near an intersection.
- At or near a railroad crossing.
- On or just before a bridge, a tunnel or a viaduct where your view is blocked.
- In a work zone where passing is not permitted.
- When a vehicle ahead has stopped at a crosswalk to let someone cross.
- When a school bus has stopped with its red lights flashing.

Many states set a fixed distance, often 100 feet, from intersections, railroad crossings and bridges where passing is banned. Chapter 2 shows the signs that mark no passing zones, and Chapter 3 covers center line markings.

> **Test trap:** Some questions describe a broken yellow line and a hill crest just ahead. The broken line does not override what you can see. If a curve or hill blocks your view of oncoming traffic, you may not pass.

## 7.4 Passing on the Right

In most cases you pass on the left. Passing on the right is allowed only in a few situations, and only when you can do it safely:

- The driver ahead is turning left and you can go around on the right without leaving the paved road.
- You are on a road with two or more lanes going your way, such as a freeway or a one way street.

You may never drive onto the shoulder, a bike lane or the edge of the road to pass, unless a sign or an officer tells you to. Drivers do not expect to be passed on the right, and people walking or on bikes may be there.

## 7.5 Being Passed

When another driver passes you, help them get by safely:

- Stay in your lane and keep to the right side of it.
- Keep a steady speed, or slow down slightly. Never speed up while someone is passing you; it is against the law in most states and it traps the passing driver in the oncoming lane.
- If the passing driver is in trouble because of oncoming traffic, slow down to let them back in ahead of you.
- At night, switch your headlights to low beam as the passing car moves alongside, so you do not blind the driver.

If many cars are passing you on the right on a multi-lane road, you are in the wrong lane. Move right when it is safe.

> **Remember it:** When you are passed, hold your lane and your speed or ease off. Never race.

## 7.6 Entering a Freeway

A **freeway** (also called an expressway or interstate) is a divided highway with no cross traffic. You can enter or leave it only at ramps called **interchanges**.

### The Parts of an Entrance

- The **on-ramp** leads you from the local road up to the freeway. Watch for a yellow advisory speed sign on a curved ramp.
- The **acceleration lane** runs beside the freeway at the end of the ramp. It gives you room to reach the speed of freeway traffic.
- The **merge area** is where the acceleration lane ends and you join the right lane.

### How to Merge

1. On the ramp, look for gaps in the freeway traffic as early as you can. Use your mirrors and quick glances over your shoulder.
2. Signal to show you are joining.
3. In the acceleration lane, speed up to match the speed of the traffic you are joining.
4. Pick your gap, check your blind spot and move smoothly into the right lane.
5. Cancel your signal and build a safe following distance.

Traffic already on the freeway usually has the right of way. Drivers in the right lane may move over to make room for you if it is safe, but you cannot count on it. Plan to fit in.

Avoid stopping at the end of the acceleration lane. Starting from a stop and joining traffic moving at 65 mph is very hard and dangerous. Stop only if there is truly no gap, then wait for a large one.

> **Test trap:** Merging slowly feels careful, but it is not. A question that asks how to enter a freeway wants you to reach the speed of traffic in the acceleration lane, then merge.

## 7.7 Driving on the Freeway

### Speed and Space

Keep up with the flow of traffic within the speed limit. Leave a larger following gap than on city streets, because speeds are higher. Look well ahead for brake lights, slowdowns and lanes that end.

### Helping Others Merge

As you near an on-ramp, watch for cars entering. If it is safe, move one lane to the left to give them room. If you cannot, adjust your speed a little so they can fit in ahead of or behind you.

### Fatigue and Highway Hypnosis

Long, straight freeway drives can lull you into a dull, half-awake state sometimes called **highway hypnosis**. Your eyes are open, but you notice less. Keep your eyes moving, check your mirrors often, take a break at least every two hours, and stop driving if you feel your eyes closing. After a long stretch at high speed, 45 mph can feel like a crawl. Check your speedometer when you leave the freeway so you do not carry too much speed onto the ramp.

## 7.8 Leaving a Freeway

### Plan Early

Know your exit number and name before you get on the freeway. Guide signs tell you about an exit well before you reach it. Move into the lane for your exit early, one lane at a time.

### Use the Deceleration Lane

Many exits have a **deceleration lane**: an extra lane on the right that leads to the off-ramp. Signal several seconds before the exit, move into the deceleration lane, and do your slowing down there, not in the freeway lane. Then follow the advisory speed on the ramp. Most exits are on the right, but some are on the left, so read the signs.

### If You Miss Your Exit

Go on to the next exit. Never stop, back up or turn around on a freeway, and never cut across the painted area between the ramp and the freeway. Missing an exit costs you a few minutes. Backing up on a freeway can cost lives.

> **Remember it:** Missed your exit? Take the next one. Never back up on a freeway.

## 7.9 HOV, Express and Toll Lanes

### HOV Lanes

A **high occupancy vehicle (HOV) lane**, often called a carpool lane, is reserved for vehicles carrying a minimum number of people, such as two or more. The lane is marked with a white **diamond** painted on the pavement and with signs. A sign such as HOV 2+ means at least two people must be in the vehicle, counting the driver. The signs also show the hours the rule applies. Outside those hours, the lane may be open to everyone.

Some HOV lanes are separated from the other lanes by a barrier, a painted buffer or a double solid white line. Enter and leave only where the lines are broken or where signs show an opening. Crossing the solid lines is against the law.

Buses, and in many places motorcycles, may use HOV lanes. Some states also allow certain clean-fuel vehicles with a special decal.

### Express and Toll Lanes

An **express lane** runs next to the regular lanes and charges a toll to use it. In some, carpools ride free or pay less. The price may change with traffic, and signs show the current price as you approach. You usually pay through an electronic transponder or by a photo of your plate. Purple on a sign points to lanes that only drivers with an electronic toll account may use.

## 7.10 Breakdowns and Emergencies on the Highway

If your car starts to fail on a highway, act early, while you still have speed and control.

1. Turn on your **hazard lights** (the flashers).
2. Check your mirrors, signal and steer onto the right shoulder, as far from traffic as you can. If you can reach an exit or a wide spot safely, use it.
3. Stop with your wheels turned slightly away from traffic, set the parking brake and turn off the engine if the problem allows it.
4. Get help: call for roadside assistance or, if anyone is hurt or the car is in a dangerous spot, call 911. Use mile markers and the last exit to tell them where you are.

### Stay In or Get Out?

In most breakdowns on a busy highway, staying in the car with your seat belt on is the safer choice. Cars on the shoulder do get hit, but people standing beside them are hurt far worse. Get out only if the car is on fire, is smoking, is in a lane of traffic, or you can move to a safe place well away from the road, such as behind a guardrail. Leave through the door on the side away from traffic.

If you have warning triangles or flares and it is safe, place them behind your car to warn approaching drivers. Raise the hood or tie a light colored cloth to the antenna or door handle as a sign you need help.

### Blowouts and Stalls in Traffic

If a tire blows out, hold the wheel firmly, keep the car straight, ease off the gas and do not brake hard. Slow down gradually and steer to the shoulder once you have control. Chapter 10 covers tire, brake and other emergencies in detail.

> **Road tip:** When you see a stopped car, tow truck or emergency vehicle on the shoulder, move over a lane if you can, or slow down if you cannot. Chapter 4 covers the Move Over law.

## Quick Review

- Keep right except to pass; use the left lane to pass or turn left.
- For every lane change: mirrors, signal, head check, then move smoothly.
- Cross several lanes one at a time.
- Pass on the left only with a broken line on your side and a clear view far ahead; never on hills, curves, or near intersections, railroad crossings and bridges.
- Pass on the right only when a car ahead is turning left or on a road with two or more lanes your way, and never on the shoulder.
- When you are being passed, hold your lane and do not speed up.
- Reach the speed of traffic in the acceleration lane, then merge into a gap.
- Slow down in the deceleration lane; if you miss your exit, go to the next one.
- A white diamond marks an HOV lane; obey the occupancy and hours on the signs.
- If you break down, get onto the shoulder, turn on your hazard lights and usually stay buckled in.
