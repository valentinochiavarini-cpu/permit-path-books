# Chapter 10: Night, Weather and Emergencies

Darkness and bad weather do not change the rules, but they shrink what you can see and stretch the distance you need to stop. The fix is almost always the same: slow down and add space. Emergencies give you one or two seconds to act, so learn the right moves now.

## 10.1 Headlights and Night Driving

Turn on your headlights at night, from around sunset to sunrise (the box below gives your state's exact times), and any time rain, snow, fog, smoke or dusk makes it hard to see far ahead or for others to see you. **Daytime running lights are not enough:** on many cars they light only the front, and the taillights stay dark. Low beams in poor weather are always legal and make your car easier to see. ### High Beams and Low Beams

Use **high beams** on dark open roads when no one is near. Switch to **low beams**:

- when a vehicle is coming toward you, well before it gets close;
- when you come up behind another vehicle, so your lights do not glare in its mirrors;
- in fog, heavy rain or snow, where high beams bounce back and make it harder to see.

Most states set a distance for each case, and the distance behind a vehicle you are following is shorter than the distance for oncoming traffic. The box below gives your state's rule.

If an oncoming driver keeps high beams on, flash your own high beams once, quickly, as a reminder. If the lights stay bright, do not stare into them. Look down and to the right, at the white edge line, to stay in your lane, and slow down if the glare is bad. For glare from behind, flip the day and night tab on your inside mirror.

### Do Not Outdrive Your Headlights

If you cannot stop within the distance your lights show you, you are **overdriving your headlights**. Low beams light only a few hundred feet ahead, so on dark roads slow down enough to stop for anything at the edge of your lights.

## 10.2 Rain and Wet Roads

Roads are most slippery in the first few minutes of a rain after a dry spell, when oil and dirt on the pavement mix with the water into a greasy film.

In rain, slow down, add at least 2 seconds to your following distance (more in heavy rain) and turn on your low beams. Do not use cruise control on wet or icy roads: if the car starts to slide, cruise control can keep feeding power to the wheels.

### Hydroplaning

In **hydroplaning**, your tires ride on a layer of water instead of the road, and you lose steering and braking. It can start at speeds as low as 30 to 35 mph, and the risk grows with speed, worn tread and low tire pressure.

![On a wet road the tread pushes water aside, but at speed a wedge of water can build up and lift the tire off the pavement.](fig/hydroplaning.svg)

If the steering suddenly feels light or the car does not respond, ease your foot off the gas and do not brake hard. Hold the wheel straight, or steer gently only where you need to go, until the tires grip again.

### Flooded Roads

Never drive into water flowing across a road. About a foot of moving water can carry away most cars, and the road under it may be washed out. Turn around and find another way. After driving through water deep enough to soak your brakes, press the brake pedal lightly a few times while you drive slowly, to dry them.

## 10.3 Fog, Smoke, Dust and Wind

- Slow down until you can stop within the distance you can see.
- Use **low beams**, and fog lights if your car has them. High beams reflect off the fog and blind you.
- Use the right edge line as a guide. Do not follow the taillights of the car ahead: if it leaves the road or stops, you will follow it.

If you cannot see well enough to drive, do not stop in a travel lane. Signal, pull as far off the road as you can, turn on your hazard flashers, and wait for the fog to lift.

Strong side winds push your car out of its lane, most of all on bridges and in open country. Slow down, keep both hands on the wheel and give trucks and trailers extra room.

## 10.4 Snow and Ice

Before you drive, start the engine and give it a few minutes to warm up so the defroster can work. Clear snow and ice from every window, the mirrors, the lights and the roof, where it can slide down over your windshield or fly into the car behind.

- Start, steer and brake gently. If the wheels spin when you start, ease off the gas.
- Drive well below the posted limit. On packed snow, slow to half your normal speed or less. Use a lower gear on slippery hills.
- On ice, you can need up to about ten times the distance to stop that you need on dry pavement. Leave a gap many times your normal one, and slow to a crawl.
- **Bridges, overpasses and shaded spots** freeze first, because cold air passes over and under them.

**Black ice** is a thin, clear layer of ice that looks like wet pavement. If the road looks wet but you see no spray from the tires of cars ahead, suspect ice.

Four-wheel drive and all-wheel drive help you get moving on snow. They do not help you stop or turn on ice.

In winter, carry a scraper, a blanket, a flashlight and a phone charger. If you must wait in a car stuck in snow, keep the exhaust pipe clear and crack a window, because carbon monoxide can build up inside.

## 10.5 Skids

A **skid** happens when your tires lose their grip, usually from too much speed, hard braking or sharp turning.

1. Take your foot off the gas. If you braked hard and the wheels locked, ease off the brake so they can roll again.
2. Look where you want the car to go, not at what you are trying to avoid.
3. Steer gently in that direction. If the rear of the car slides to the right, that means steering to the right.
4. Be ready for the back end to swing the other way as the tires catch, and steer gently back.

![When the rear slides right, ease off the gas, look down your lane where you want to go and steer gently right.](fig/skid_recovery.svg)

## 10.6 Emergency Stops and Swerves

Keep both hands on the wheel at about **9 and 3** or **8 and 4** o'clock. You can turn quickly from there, and your arms stay clear of the airbag.

**Antilock brakes (ABS)** stop the wheels from locking in a hard stop so you can still steer. In an emergency with ABS, press the brake pedal hard and keep pressing. A pulsing pedal is normal. Do not pump the pedal.

Without ABS, pressing hard can lock the wheels, and a car with locked wheels cannot be steered. Use **threshold braking**: press firmly to just short of locking. If the wheels lock, ease off a little, then press again. Some manuals describe this as pumping the brakes. Either way, the wheels must keep rolling for you to steer.

At speeds above about 25 mph, you can often steer around a hazard in less distance than you need to stop. Look for an escape path, usually to the right, and steer firmly but not sharply.

> **Remember it:** With ABS: stomp, stay, steer.

If a vehicle comes toward you in your lane, sound your horn, slow down and steer to the right, onto the shoulder if you must. Do not swerve left: the other driver may swing back into that lane. If a crash cannot be avoided, keep braking. Every mile per hour you shed makes the impact less severe.

## 10.7 Vehicle Emergencies

### Tire Blowout

A burst tire makes the car pull hard toward the side of the flat. Hold the wheel firmly with both hands and keep the car pointed straight. Do not brake hard: ease off the gas and let the car slow down. Once you are slow and under control, brake gently, signal and pull off the road, and turn on your hazard flashers.

### Brake Failure

If the brake pedal suddenly sinks to the floor:

1. Pump the brake pedal fast and hard. This may build up enough pressure to stop.
2. If that does not work, apply the parking brake slowly and steadily. Pulling it hard can lock the rear wheels.
3. Shift to a lower gear so the engine helps slow the car.
4. Steer to a safe place off the road, and warn others with your horn and hazard flashers.

Do not drive the car again until the brakes have been repaired.

### Stuck Accelerator

1. Shift to **neutral**. The engine will race, but the wheels will no longer be driven.
2. Brake firmly and steer to a safe place off the road.
3. When you are stopped, turn off the engine.

Do not turn the engine off while you are still moving unless neutral does not work. On many cars, that also turns off the power steering and power brakes, and turning the key too far can lock the steering wheel.

### Running Off the Pavement

If your right wheels drop off the pavement onto the shoulder, do not jerk the wheel to get back on; that can throw the car across the road into oncoming traffic. Hold the wheel firmly, keep going straight and ease off the gas. When you have slowed down and the lane is clear, steer gently back onto the pavement.

### Engine Failure, Fire and Breakdowns

If the engine dies while you are moving, shift to neutral, signal and steer off the road while the car still rolls. Steering and braking will feel heavy, so use more force.

If you see smoke from under the hood, pull over and turn off the engine. Get everyone out and at least about 100 feet away, and call 911. Do not open the hood, because fresh air can feed the fire.

For any breakdown, get the car as far off the road as you can and turn on your hazard flashers. Raise the hood to show you need help only if you can do it without stepping into traffic. Then wait in the car with your belt on, or well away from traffic. Chapter 7 covers highway breakdowns.

## 10.8 After a Crash

1. **Stop.** Every driver involved in a crash must stop at the scene or as close to it as is safe. Leaving the scene of a crash with injuries is a serious crime.
2. **Protect the scene.** Turn on your hazard flashers. If no one is hurt and the cars can be driven, move them out of the travel lanes to the shoulder or a nearby lot. [[generic]]Many states require this for a minor crash.[[/generic]]
3. **Check for injuries and call 911** if anyone is hurt, if a car cannot be moved, or if the road is blocked. Do not move an injured person unless there is an immediate danger such as fire.
4. **Exchange information** with the other driver: names, addresses, driver's license numbers, license plate numbers, insurance company and policy numbers, and the vehicle owner's name if it is different.
5. **Collect facts:** place, time, photos, witness names and numbers.
6. **Report the crash** to the police and the licensing agency when the law requires it, and tell your insurance company.

If you hit a parked car with no one in it, or damage a fence, a sign or other property, you still have to stop. Try to find the owner. If you cannot, leave a note in a safe, visible place, such as under a windshield wiper, with your name, address and phone number and a short account of what happened. Then report it to the police. Driving away without leaving your information is a hit and run.

## 10.9 If Police Signal You to Stop

Flashing red and blue lights or a siren behind you mean pull over. Turn on your right signal, slow down and stop on the right side of the road or the shoulder as soon as it is safe. If there is no safe spot, keep driving slowly to the nearest one.

- Stay in the car unless the officer tells you to get out. Turn off the engine and the radio.
- At night, turn on the interior light so the officer can see inside.
- Keep your hands on the steering wheel where the officer can see them, and keep your seat belt on.
- Reach for your license, registration and proof of insurance only when the officer asks, and say where they are before you reach.
- Stay calm and polite. If you disagree with a ticket, do not argue at the roadside; you can contest it in court.

## Quick Review

- Headlights at night and in poor visibility; low beams for oncoming cars and when following.
- Roads are slickest in the first minutes of rain; ice can take ten times the distance to stop.
- Skid: ease off the gas and steer where you want to go. With ABS, press hard and hold.
- Car coming at you in your lane: horn, slow down, steer right. Stuck accelerator: neutral, brake, pull off.
- Stopped by police: pull over right, stay in the car, hands on the wheel, belt on.
