# Chapter 10: Night, Weather and Emergencies

Most of your practice driving happens on dry roads in daylight. Then one evening it rains, the light fades and the road ahead turns black and shiny. Darkness and bad weather do not change the rules, but they shrink what you can see and stretch the distance you need to stop. The fix is almost always the same: slow down and add space.

Emergencies are rarer, but they come with no warning. A tire bursts, the brake pedal sinks to the floor, the car starts to slide. You will have one or two seconds to act, and there is no time to work out what to do. Drivers who have pictured the right moves ahead of time react calmly. Drivers who have not tend to slam the brakes and jerk the wheel, which usually makes things worse.

The knowledge test asks about all of this in short scenarios. Learn the one or two right moves for each situation in this chapter, and you will have them ready on the test and on the road.

> **On the test**
>
> - When to use headlights, and when to switch from high beams to low beams.
> - What lights to use in fog, and what to do when you cannot see.
> - Why roads are most slippery when rain first starts, and what hydroplaning is.
> - How to recover from a skid and how to brake with and without antilock brakes.
> - What to do for a blowout, brake failure or a stuck accelerator.
> - Your duties after a crash, including a crash with a parked car.

## 10.1 Driving at Night

At night you see less, you see it later, and you judge distance poorly. Glare from other cars adds to the problem. About half of all traffic deaths happen in the dark, even though far fewer miles are driven then.

### When to Turn On Your Headlights

Turn on your headlights whenever it is dark, and do not wait until it is fully dark. Most states require headlights from shortly after sunset until shortly before sunrise, whenever you cannot see clearly a set distance ahead, and many states also require them whenever your wipers are on because of rain, snow or fog. Using low beams in the daytime in any poor weather is always legal and makes your car easier to see.

Running lights alone are not enough. On many cars, daytime running lights do not turn on your taillights. If your dashboard is lit but the road ahead seems dim, check that your headlights are actually on.

### High Beams and Low Beams

**Low beams** light the road a short distance ahead and are aimed down and to the right so they do not blind other drivers. Use them in town, in traffic, and in fog, rain and snow.

**High beams** reach much farther. Use them on dark open roads when no one is near, so you can spot people, animals and curves sooner.

Switch to low beams:

- when a vehicle is coming toward you, well before it gets close;
- when you come up behind another vehicle, so your lights do not glare in its mirrors;
- in fog, heavy rain or snow, where high beams bounce back and make it harder to see.

The law sets a distance for each case, and the distance behind a vehicle you are following is shorter than the distance for oncoming traffic.

### When Another Driver Does Not Dim

If an oncoming driver keeps high beams on, do not flash yours back at full strength and do not stare into the lights. Look down and to the right, at the white edge line, and use it to stay in your lane until the car passes. Slow down if the glare is bad. For glare from behind, flip the day and night tab on your inside mirror, or tilt it slightly.

### Do Not Outdrive Your Headlights

Low beams light only a few hundred feet ahead, about the distance you need to stop from highway speed on dry pavement. If you drive so fast that you cannot stop within the distance your lights show you, you are **overdriving your headlights**. On dark roads, slow down enough that you could stop for anything that appears at the edge of your lights.

### Keeping Your Eyes and Car Ready

Keep your windshield clean inside and out; a film on the glass scatters oncoming headlights into glare. Keep headlight lenses clean too. Dim the dashboard lights so your eyes stay adjusted to the dark. Fatigue is worse at night, and Chapter 11 covers drowsy driving.

## 10.2 Rain and Wet Roads

### The First Minutes Are the Worst

Roads are most slippery in the first few minutes of a rain after a dry spell. Oil, rubber dust and dirt on the pavement float up and mix with the water into a greasy film. Heavier rain later washes some of it away, but by then puddles and standing water bring a new risk.

In rain, slow down, double your following distance at least, and turn on your low beams so others see you. Do not use cruise control on wet roads: if the car starts to slide, cruise control can keep feeding power to the wheels.

### Hydroplaning

**Hydroplaning** happens when a layer of water builds up between your tires and the road, and the tires ride on the water like water skis. You lose steering and braking. It can start at speeds as low as about 35 mph, and the risk grows quickly with speed, with worn tread and with low tire pressure.

![On a wet road the tread pushes water aside, but at speed a wedge of water can build up and lift the tire off the pavement.](fig/hydroplaning.svg)

Signs that you are hydroplaning: the steering suddenly feels light, the engine speeds up a little, or the car does not respond when you turn the wheel.

If it happens:

- Ease your foot off the gas. Do not brake hard.
- Hold the wheel straight, or steer gently only where you need to go.
- Wait for the tires to grip the road again. You will feel the steering firm up.

The best defense is to slow down in heavy rain, keep your tires inflated and replace them before the tread gets thin. Drive in the tire tracks of the car ahead, where there is less water.

### Flooded Roads

Never drive into water flowing across a road, and avoid standing water when you cannot see the bottom. The National Weather Service warns that about a foot of moving water can carry away most cars, and about two feet can carry away an SUV or pickup. The road under the water may also be washed out. Turn around and find another way.

If you drive through a puddle deep enough to soak your brakes, they may not work well afterward. Press the brake pedal lightly a few times while you drive slowly, to dry them.

> **Test trap:** A question may ask when a road is most slippery. Many people pick "after hours of heavy rain." The expected answer is "during the first few minutes of rain," when oil on the road mixes with the water.

## 10.3 Fog, Smoke and Dust

Fog cuts your view to a few car lengths and makes speed hard to judge. Smoke from fires and blowing dust do the same.

- Slow down before you enter the fog, and keep slowing until you can stop within the distance you can see.
- Use **low beams**, and fog lights if your car has them. High beams reflect off the fog and blind you.
- Use the right edge line as a guide. Do not drive by following the taillights of the car ahead: if it leaves the road or stops, you will follow it.
- Leave extra following distance, and do not pass.
- Turn off the radio and lower your window slightly so you can hear traffic you cannot see.

If the fog gets so thick that you cannot see well enough to drive, do not stop in a travel lane. Signal, pull as far off the road as you can, away from the travel lanes, turn on your hazard flashers, and wait for the fog to lift. If you can get to a parking lot or rest area, that is safer still.

## 10.4 Snow and Ice

### Before You Go

Clear all snow and ice from every window, the mirrors, the lights and the roof before you drive. A small hole in the windshield frost is not enough, and snow sliding off your roof can blind you or the driver behind you. Start slowly and accelerate gently so the wheels do not spin.

### On the Road

Everything takes longer on snow and ice: speeding up, turning and stopping. Do each one slowly and smoothly, one at a time.

- Leave a much larger following distance than on dry roads, about 8 to 10 seconds on ice.
- Brake early and gently. Steer gently.
- Do not use cruise control.
- **Bridges, overpasses and shaded spots** freeze first and stay icy after the rest of the road has melted, because cold air passes over and under them.

**Black ice** is a thin, clear layer of ice that looks like wet pavement. It forms most often near freezing, early in the morning, on bridges and in shady spots. If the road looks wet but you see no spray from the tires of cars ahead, suspect ice.

Four-wheel drive and all-wheel drive help you get moving on snow. They do not help you stop or turn on ice. A heavy vehicle with four-wheel drive needs just as much room to stop as any other car.

If you get stuck, keep the wheels straight and rock the car gently forward and back. Spinning the wheels fast only digs you in deeper. If you must wait in a stuck car, keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow and crack a window, because carbon monoxide can build up inside the car.

> **Road tip:** In freezing weather, bridges can be slick when the rest of the road is fine. Ease off the gas before the bridge, cross it with the wheel steady, and brake before or after, not on it.

## 10.5 Wind

Strong side winds push your car out of its lane, most of all on bridges, in open country and when you come out from behind a hill or a large truck. Tall, light vehicles such as vans and cars pulling trailers are pushed the most.

Slow down, keep both hands on the wheel, and be ready to correct with small steering moves. Leave extra room around trucks, campers and trailers, which can be blown into your lane.

## 10.6 Skids

A **skid** happens when your tires lose their grip and the car slides. Most skids come from going too fast for the conditions, braking too hard or turning the wheel too sharply.

### Recovering from a Skid

1. Take your foot off the gas. Do not slam the brakes.
2. Look where you want the car to go, not at what you are trying to avoid. Your hands tend to follow your eyes.
3. Steer gently in that direction. If the rear of the car slides to the right, that means steering to the right.
4. Be ready for the back end to swing the other way as the tires catch, and steer gently back. You may need to do this more than once.

![When the rear slides right, ease off the gas, look down your lane where you want to go and steer gently right.](fig/skid_recovery.svg)

### Braking: With and Without Antilock Brakes

Most cars today have **antilock brakes (ABS)**, which stop the wheels from locking in a hard stop so you can still steer. In an emergency with ABS, press the brake pedal hard and keep pressing. You may feel the pedal pulse and hear a grinding or buzzing sound. That is normal. Do not pump the pedal, and steer around the hazard if you need to.

Without ABS, pressing hard can lock the wheels, and a car with locked wheels cannot be steered. Press firmly just short of locking. If the wheels lock, ease off a little, then press again. Your owner's manual says whether your car has ABS, and a dashboard ABS light comes on briefly when you start the car.

| Brakes | In a hard stop | Pump the pedal? |
|---|---|---|
| With ABS | Press hard, hold it, steer | No |
| Without ABS | Press firmly, ease off if wheels lock | Ease and reapply |

> **Remember it:** With ABS: stomp, stay, steer.

## 10.7 Vehicle Emergencies

### Tire Blowout

When a tire bursts you will hear a bang, and the car will pull hard toward the side of the flat tire. With a rear tire, the back end may sway.

- Hold the wheel firmly with both hands and keep the car pointed straight.
- Do not brake hard. Ease your foot off the gas and let the car slow down.
- Once you are slow and under control, brake gently, signal and pull off the road.
- Turn on your hazard flashers. Change the tire only if you are well away from traffic; otherwise call for help.

### Brake Failure

If the brake pedal suddenly sinks to the floor or the car will not slow down:

1. Pump the brake pedal fast and hard. This may build up enough pressure to stop.
2. If that does not work, apply the parking brake slowly and steadily. Pulling it hard can lock the rear wheels and start a skid.
3. Shift to a lower gear so the engine helps slow the car.
4. Look for a safe place to steer off the road: a shoulder, an open lot, an uphill side road. Warn others with your horn and hazard flashers.

Once you stop, do not drive the car again until the brakes have been repaired.

### Stuck Accelerator

If the gas pedal sticks and the car keeps speeding up:

1. Shift to **neutral**. The engine will race, but the wheels will no longer be driven.
2. Brake firmly and steer to a safe place off the road.
3. When you are stopped, turn off the engine.

Do not turn the engine off while you are still moving unless shifting to neutral does not work. On many cars, turning off the engine also turns off the power steering and power brakes, which makes the car much harder to steer and stop, and turning the key too far can lock the steering wheel.

If a floor mat is jammed on top of the pedal, you may be able to hook it out of the way with your toe, but keep your eyes on the road and do not reach down with your hand.

### Running Off the Pavement

If your right wheels drop off the edge of the pavement onto a soft or lower shoulder, do not jerk the wheel to get back on. That can throw the car across the road into oncoming traffic.

- Hold the wheel firmly and keep going straight along the shoulder.
- Ease off the gas. Brake gently only if you need to.
- When you have slowed down and the lane is clear, turn the wheel gently to come back onto the pavement, then straighten out.

### Engine Failure, Power Loss and Fire

If the engine dies while you are moving, shift to neutral, signal and steer off the road while the car still rolls. Steering and braking will feel heavy, so use more force. Turn on your hazard flashers.

If you see smoke from under the hood or smell burning, pull over and turn off the engine. Get everyone out and well away from the car, at least about 100 feet, and call 911. Do not open the hood, because fresh air can feed the fire.

### Stopping Safely on the Road

When your car breaks down, get it as far off the road as you can, ideally past the shoulder. Turn on your hazard flashers. On a highway, stay in the car with your seat belt on if you are on a narrow shoulder with traffic flying past, and call for help. Get out only on the side away from traffic and only if you can stand well away from the road. Chapter 7 covers highway breakdowns.

## 10.8 After a Crash

A crash is frightening even when no one is hurt. Knowing the steps keeps you from making it worse and keeps you within the law.

### Your Duties at the Scene

1. **Stop.** Every state requires a driver involved in a crash to stop at the scene or as close to it as is safe. Leaving the scene of a crash with injuries is a serious crime.
2. **Protect the scene.** Turn on your hazard flashers. If no one is hurt and the cars can be driven, move them out of the travel lanes to the shoulder or a nearby lot. Many states require this for a minor crash. If you cannot move the car, stay inside with your belt on, or get well away from traffic.
3. **Check for injuries and call 911.** Call if anyone is hurt, if a car cannot be moved, or if the road is blocked. Do not move an injured person unless there is an immediate danger such as fire.
4. **Exchange information** with the other driver: names, addresses, driver's license numbers, license plate numbers, insurance company and policy numbers, and the vehicle owner's name if it is different. Show your license and insurance card if asked.
5. **Collect facts.** Note the place and time, take photos of the cars and the scene, and get the names and phone numbers of any witnesses.
6. **Report the crash** to the police and to your state agency when the law requires it, and tell your insurance company.

Stay calm and polite. Do not argue about who caused the crash. Stick to the facts when you talk to the police.

### Hitting an Unattended Vehicle or Property

If you hit a parked car with no one in it, or damage a fence, a sign or other property, you still have to stop. Try to find the owner. If you cannot, leave a note in a safe, visible place, such as under a windshield wiper, with your name, address and phone number and a short account of what happened. Then report it to the police as your state requires. Driving away without leaving your information is a hit and run.

### Coming Up on a Crash

If you are not involved, do not stop in the road to look. Slow down, watch for people and debris, and keep moving. If you stop to help, park well off the road, turn on your hazard flashers, call 911 and let trained responders take over when they arrive. Move over or slow down for emergency vehicles on the shoulder, as Chapter 4 explains.

> **Test trap:** "If the other driver says no one needs to call anyone, you can leave without exchanging information." That is never correct. You must stop and exchange information in every crash with another vehicle, and some crashes must be reported no matter what the other driver says.

## Quick Review

- Switch to low beams for oncoming cars and when following another vehicle.
- If oncoming lights blind you, look toward the right edge line.
- Do not drive faster than you can stop within the reach of your headlights.
- Roads are slickest in the first few minutes of rain.
- If you hydroplane, ease off the gas and do not brake hard.
- In fog use low beams; if you cannot see, pull fully off the road and turn on your flashers.
- Bridges and overpasses freeze first.
- In a skid, ease off the gas, look where you want to go and steer there.
- With ABS, press hard and hold; do not pump.
- Stuck accelerator: shift to neutral, brake, pull off, then turn off the engine.
