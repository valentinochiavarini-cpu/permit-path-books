# Chapter 3: Signals, Lights and Pavement Markings

Signs tell you the rules for a stretch of road. Signals and pavement markings tell you what to do right now: stop here, go now, stay in this lane, do not cross this line. They change from block to block and sometimes from second to second, so you have to read them on the move.

The knowledge test leans hard on this chapter. Expect questions about what a flashing light means, what to do when the light turns yellow, which lines you may cross to pass, and what a red X over a lane means. Many of these questions describe a picture in words, so you need to know the colors and patterns by name.

Like road signs, signals and markings follow one national standard. A double yellow line means the same thing in every state. The few rules that change from state to state, such as turning on red, are marked with a box for your state.

> **On the test**
>
> - Know what each steady, flashing and arrow light means, including the flashing yellow arrow.
> - Know what to do at a signal that is dark or not working.
> - Read pedestrian signals and the countdown display.
> - Tell yellow lines from white lines, and solid lines from broken ones.
> - Know when you may cross a line, use a center turn lane or drive in a reversible lane.

## 3.1 How a Traffic Signal Works

A **traffic signal** is a set of lights that assigns turns at an intersection. Most signals stack three lights in a column, red on top, yellow in the middle and green on the bottom. Signals laid out side to side put red on the left. The order matters: if you are color blind, or the sun is behind the signal, the position of the lit lamp still tells you what it says.

Each light can appear in three ways:

- **Steady**: the light stays on.
- **Flashing**: the light blinks on and off.
- **Arrow**: the light is shaped like an arrow and controls only the movement the arrow points to.

A **circular** light (a plain round lamp) applies to every movement from your lane unless an arrow next to it says otherwise.

## 3.2 Steady Lights

### Steady Green

A **steady green light** means you may go straight or turn, but it does not mean the way is clear. You still yield to pedestrians in the crosswalk and to vehicles that are already in the intersection. If you are turning left on a steady green, you also yield to oncoming traffic that is close enough to be a danger. That turn is called an **unprotected left turn**, and Chapter 5 covers it step by step.

Do not enter on green if traffic ahead is backed up and you would be stuck in the middle of the intersection when the light changes. Blocking the box stops cross traffic and can earn you a ticket.

### Steady Yellow

A **steady yellow light** means the green is ending and a red light is coming next. It is a warning, not an invitation to speed up. Stop if you can do it safely before the stop line. If you are so close that a hard stop would be dangerous, or you are already in the intersection, continue through with care.

> **Road tip:** As you approach a green light that has been on for a while, ask yourself one question: "If it turns yellow now, will I stop or go?" Pick your answer before you reach the point of no return, and the yellow will never surprise you.

### Steady Red

A **steady red light** means stop. Your stopping point is the white stop line when one is painted, the near edge of the crosswalk when there is no line, and the edge of the intersection when there is neither. Stay stopped until the light turns green, unless a turn on red is allowed and you have checked that it is safe.

### Turning on Red

Every state lets you turn right on a steady red light after a complete stop, unless a sign says NO TURN ON RED. Before you turn, yield to pedestrians and to drivers who have the green. Most states also permit a left turn on red when both the street you are on and the street you are entering carry one way traffic, under the same conditions. Some cities and some states add their own limits.

## 3.3 Flashing Lights

Flashing lights are used at quiet intersections, at night, and when a signal is being repaired. They work like signs.

- **Flashing red**: treat it exactly like a stop sign. Come to a complete stop, yield to anyone with the right of way, then go when it is safe.
- **Flashing yellow**: slow down and go ahead with care. You do not have to stop, but watch for cross traffic and pedestrians.

The national standard gives a flashing green light no meaning and does not allow it on traffic signals, so it will not appear as a correct answer on the test.

> **Remember it:** Flashing red is a stop sign made of light. Flashing yellow is a caution sign made of light.

## 3.4 Arrow Lights

An arrow light controls only the move it points to. The other lights on the signal control the other moves.

| Arrow | What it means for that turn |
|---|---|
| Steady green arrow | Go. Oncoming traffic is stopped |
| Steady yellow arrow | The protected turn is ending |
| Flashing yellow arrow | Turn allowed, but yield first |
| Steady red arrow | Do not make that turn |
| Flashing red arrow | Stop, then turn when safe |

A **green arrow** gives you a **protected turn**: traffic that would cross your path is held by a red light. You still yield to any pedestrian or vehicle that is already in the intersection.

A **flashing yellow arrow** is the most important arrow to know. It means you may turn left, but oncoming traffic has a green light. You must wait for a safe gap in oncoming traffic and for pedestrians to clear the crosswalk. It is the same unprotected turn you make on a steady green ball, with a light that reminds you to yield.

A **red arrow** means you may not make that turn yet. Wait for a green arrow or a green light. States differ on whether you may ever turn on a red arrow after a full stop, so the safe habit is to treat a red arrow as a firm no unless your state box above says otherwise.

> **Test trap:** The flashing yellow arrow does not give you the right of way. It tells you that you may turn only after you yield to oncoming traffic and pedestrians.

## 3.5 When the Signal Is Dark or Broken

Storms and power failures sometimes knock out a signal. If you come to an intersection where the signal is completely dark, treat it as an **all way stop**. Stop, take your turn in the order cars arrived, and go only when it is safe. Chapter 4 explains the order at an all way stop.

If the signal is stuck on one color for a very long time, or the lights are flickering in a pattern that makes no sense, slow down, be ready to stop and treat the intersection with the same caution.

A police officer or another person who is legally directing traffic overrides the signal. If an officer waves you through a red light, go. If an officer holds up a hand while your light is green, stop.

## 3.6 Lane Control Signals and Ramp Meters

### Lane Control Signals

Some roads change the direction or use of a lane during the day. On those roads, **lane control signals** hang over each lane. They are rectangular signals, and they tell you whether you may drive in the lane beneath them.

| Light over the lane | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Green arrow pointing down | You may drive in this lane |
| Steady yellow X | Leave this lane safely; it is about to close |
| White two way left turn arrow | Use this lane only to turn left; drivers from both directions share it |
| Flashing yellow X (some states only) | Use this lane only to turn left |
| Steady red X | Do not drive in this lane |

You will see these signals at toll plazas, in tunnels, on bridges and over reversible lanes (section 3.10). A red X often means traffic in that lane is coming toward you, so never drive under one.

### Ramp Meters

A **ramp meter** is a small signal on a freeway on ramp, usually with red and green lights. It lets cars onto the freeway one at a time so the merge stays smooth. Stop at the line when the light is red. When it turns green, go: in most cases only one car goes on each green. A sign may say how many cars may go per green. If the meter is dark, use the ramp normally.

## 3.7 Pedestrian Signals

**Pedestrian signals** are the lights on the corners that tell people walking when to cross. You need to read them because they also tell you what the people around your car are about to do.

- **Walking person** (or the word WALK), shown in white: people may start to cross.
- **Flashing raised hand** (or flashing DONT WALK), shown in orange: people should not start to cross. Anyone already in the street should keep going to the other side.
- **Steady raised hand**, shown in orange: people should not enter the street.

Many signals add a **countdown**, a number that shows how many seconds are left for people to finish crossing. The number counts down while the hand flashes.

A flashing or steady hand does not remove a pedestrian's right of way once the person is in the crosswalk. If someone is still crossing when your light turns green, wait until the person is out of your path.

> **Road tip:** The countdown helps you too. If you are approaching a green light and the walk countdown shows 3, your light is likely to turn yellow very soon. Ease off and be ready to stop.

## 3.8 Pavement Lines: Color First

Lines painted on the road guide you between signs and signals. Read their color first, then their pattern.

- **Yellow lines** separate traffic moving in **opposite** directions. If there is a yellow line to your left, oncoming traffic is on the other side of it.
- **White lines** separate lanes moving in the **same** direction. They also mark the right edge of the road.

> **Remember it:** Yellow means traffic coming the other way. White means traffic going your way.

If you are ever unsure whether you have turned the wrong way onto a one way road or a ramp, look at the lines. On a one way road or a divided highway, the yellow line belongs on your left and the white line on your right. If the yellow line is on your right, you are going the wrong way.

## 3.9 Line Patterns: Solid, Broken and Double

The pattern of a line tells you whether you may cross it.

- A **broken line** (short dashes with gaps) means you may cross it when it is safe, to pass or to change lanes.
- A **solid line** means crossing is discouraged or not allowed.
- A **double line** means stronger limits.
- A **dotted line** (short dashes close together) extends a lane line through an intersection or warns that a lane will end or turn off ahead.

### Center Lines on Two Way Roads

| Center line | What you may do |
|---|---|
| Single broken yellow | Pass when safe, from either side |
| Solid yellow beside broken yellow | Pass only from the broken side |
| Double solid yellow | No passing from either side |

When a solid and a broken yellow line run side by side, look at the line closest to you. If the broken line is on your side, you may pass when it is safe. If the solid line is on your side, you may not.

A **double solid yellow line** means no passing for either direction. Treat it like a wall. You may cross it to turn left into or out of a driveway, an alley or a side road. Some states also allow a left turn across it for a U-turn, which Chapter 5 covers, and some let you cross it to pass a bicycle when the way is clear.

On roads with four or more lanes and no median, the center is usually a double solid yellow line. Two sets of double yellow lines with a wide space between them form a painted **median**. Do not drive on it or cross it.

### Lane Lines Between Lanes Going Your Way

A **broken white line** between lanes means you may change lanes when it is safe. A **solid white line** means stay in your lane; change lanes only if you must, such as to avoid a hazard. You will see solid white lines near intersections, on bridges and at the approach to exits. A **double solid white line** means you may not cross at all. It often separates a carpool lane from regular lanes.

### Edge Lines

A **solid white edge line** marks the right edge of the travel lane. The paved area beyond it is the **shoulder**, which is for stopping in an emergency, not for driving or passing. A **solid yellow edge line** marks the left edge on a divided highway, a one way road or a ramp. At night, in fog or in heavy rain, the right edge line is a good guide: steer by it rather than by the headlights coming toward you.

If the edge line angles in toward the center, the road is getting narrower ahead.

> **Test trap:** The test may ask which line separates traffic moving in opposite directions. The answer is yellow, whether it is solid, broken or double. White never separates opposing traffic.

## 3.10 Special Lanes

### Shared Center Turn Lanes

A **shared center turn lane** (also called a **two way left turn lane**) runs down the middle of some busy streets. A solid yellow line and a broken yellow line run along each side, with the broken line on the inside, and white arrows pointing left in both directions are usually painted on the pavement.

Drivers from both directions use this lane only to turn left, or to turn left out of a driveway and wait for a gap. Use it this way:

1. Signal, check your mirror and blind spot, and move into the lane shortly before your turn. Do not enter it a long distance ahead.
2. Watch for a driver from the other direction who may be using the same space.
3. Stop in the lane if you must, then turn when oncoming traffic is clear.

Never use the center turn lane to pass or as a travel lane.

### Reversible Lanes

A **reversible lane** carries traffic one way at some times of day and the other way at other times, usually to handle the morning and evening rush. Each side of the lane is marked with a **double broken yellow line**. Signs, overhead lane control signals or both tell you which way the lane runs right now. Before you enter, check the overhead signal: green arrow, you may use it; red X, you may not.

### Diamond Lanes

A white **diamond symbol** painted in a lane marks it as a reserved lane, often for carpools, buses or other vehicles named on the signs beside the road. Chapter 7 explains these lanes.

## 3.11 Stop Lines, Crosswalks and Words on the Pavement

### Stop Lines

A **stop line** is a wide solid white line painted across your lane at a stop sign or signal. Stop with the front of your car behind it. If there is no stop line, stop before the crosswalk. If there is no crosswalk, stop where you can see cross traffic, before you enter the intersection.

### Yield Lines

A **yield line** is a row of white triangles across the lane, pointing toward you, often called **shark teeth**. It goes with a yield sign. If you must yield, stop behind the triangles.

### Crosswalks

A **crosswalk** is where pedestrians cross. A marked crosswalk is shown by white lines: two parallel lines, or a ladder of wide bars for a high visibility crosswalk. Every intersection also has crosswalks that are not painted. The legal crosswalk usually continues across the street from the sidewalk, whether lines are there or not. Yield to pedestrians in marked and unmarked crosswalks.

Do not stop on a crosswalk. If traffic is backed up, wait behind it so people can cross in front of you.

### Arrows and Words

White **arrows** painted in a lane show which way you may go from that lane. A curved arrow means turn; a straight arrow means go straight; both together mean either. The word **ONLY** with an arrow means the lane must go that way. Get into the right lane before you reach the solid white line that starts at the intersection.

Words such as STOP AHEAD, SCHOOL, BUS ONLY or a speed number may also be painted on the road. They repeat or reinforce a sign. Pavement words are stretched long so they look normal from a driver's seat.

A large **X with the letters RR** painted across your lane means a railroad crossing is ahead. Chapter 5 covers what to do there.

Markings, signs and signals are designed to work together. If they ever seem to disagree, follow a police officer or flagger first, then the signal, then the sign.

## Quick Review

- Steady green means go after yielding; steady yellow means the red is coming, so stop if you safely can.
- Stop at the stop line, then before the crosswalk, then before the intersection.
- A right turn on red needs a complete stop first and is never allowed where a sign forbids it.
- Flashing red works like a stop sign; flashing yellow means go with caution.
- A flashing yellow arrow allows a left turn only after you yield to oncoming traffic and pedestrians.
- A dark signal is an all way stop.
- A red X over a lane means do not drive in it; a green arrow pointing down means you may.
- Yellow lines separate opposite directions; white lines separate lanes going your way.
- You may pass only from the side with the broken line; a double solid yellow line means no passing for anyone.
- A shared center turn lane is only for left turns, never for passing or travel.
