# Chapter 11: Alcohol, Drugs, Distraction and Fatigue

Most crashes start with a driver who is not fully there: someone who had a few drinks, glanced at a phone, or kept driving with heavy eyes. Each of them felt fine a second before the crash. The rule is the same everywhere: if your mind, eyes or hands are somewhere else, you are not driving.

## 11.1 What Alcohol Does to a Driver

Alcohol is a depressant. It slows the brain, and it starts working well before you feel drunk.

Alcohol hurts **judgment** first: your ability to decide how fast to take a curve, whether a gap is big enough, whether you are fit to drive at all. That is why a person who has been drinking so often decides they are fine. After judgment, alcohol slows your **reaction time**, then weakens **coordination** (steering smoothly, staying in your lane), **vision** (focus, night vision, judging distance) and **attention** (handling two things at once).

| BAC | What starts to slip |
|---|---|
| 0.02 | Tracking moving objects; doing two tasks at once |
| 0.05 | Coordination, steering, emergency reactions |
| 0.08 | Concentration, speed control, spotting hazards |
| 0.10 | Staying in lane, braking at the right time |
| 0.15 | Basic control of the car and attention |

At 0.08, a driver is about four times as likely to crash as a sober driver.

### What a Drink Is

A **standard drink** holds about the same amount of pure alcohol whatever the glass:

- 12 ounces of regular beer (about 5 percent alcohol)
- 5 ounces of wine (about 12 percent)
- 1.5 ounces of 80 proof liquor, one shot (about 40 percent)

![A regular beer, a glass of wine and a shot of liquor each hold about the same amount of alcohol.](fig/standard_drink.svg)

A large glass of wine, a strong craft beer or a mixed drink with two shots can hold two standard drinks or more. Beer is not the "safe" choice.

## 11.2 Blood Alcohol Concentration

**Blood alcohol concentration (BAC)** is the share of alcohol in your blood. Police measure it with a breath, blood or urine test. Your BAC depends on how much you drank and how fast, and on:

- **Body weight and size.** A smaller person reaches a higher BAC from the same drinks.
- **Food.** Food in your stomach slows how fast alcohol enters the blood. It does not stop it.
- **Medicines and other drugs**, which can multiply the effect of alcohol.

### Only Time Sobers You Up

Your liver breaks down alcohol at a slow, steady pace, roughly one standard drink per hour for many people. Nothing speeds that up. Black coffee, a cold shower, fresh air, exercise or a big meal after drinking do not lower your BAC.

> **Test trap:** Questions often list coffee, a cold shower or exercise as ways to sober up. The only correct answer is time.

### The Legal Limit

For drivers 21 and older, it is illegal to drive with a BAC of 0.08 or higher. [[generic]]Utah is the one exception, with a lower limit of 0.05.[[/generic]] Drivers of commercial vehicles are held to 0.04 under federal rules.

The limit is not a safe level. You can be arrested for impaired driving below it if the officer sees that alcohol has affected your driving.

### Zero Tolerance Under 21

The legal drinking age is 21. Drivers under 21 also face a **zero tolerance** law: a BAC limit of 0.02 or lower, so low that a single drink can put you over it.

## 11.3 Implied Consent and Testing

When you accept a driver's license, you agree in advance to take a chemical test (breath, blood or urine) if a police officer lawfully asks you to because they suspect impaired driving. This is **implied consent**, and every state has an implied consent law.

Refusal brings its own penalties, usually a license suspension, separate from any impaired driving charge. The refusal can also be used against you in court. Officers may also ask for roadside **field sobriety tests**, and a blood test may be used for drugs that a breath test cannot detect.

## 11.4 Impaired Driving Penalties

States call the offense **DUI**, **DWI**, **OUI** or **OVI**. The idea is the same: you may not drive while alcohol, drugs or both have affected your ability to drive safely. In every state, penalties climb with each offense and with a higher BAC. Common penalties include:

- License suspension or revocation, heavy fines and court costs.
- Jail time, above all for repeat offenses.
- An **ignition interlock**: a breath tester wired to your car's ignition. The car will not start until you blow a clean sample.
- Alcohol education or treatment, higher insurance costs and a criminal record.

An **open container** is any bottle or can of alcohol that has been opened, has a broken seal, or has some of its contents removed. [[generic]]Most states ban open containers in the passenger area of a vehicle, for the driver and for passengers.[[/generic]] If you must carry alcohol, keep it sealed and in the trunk, or behind the last row of seats in a vehicle with no trunk.

## 11.5 Drugs and Medicines

In every state, it is illegal to drive impaired by any drug: illegal drugs, marijuana, and legal medicines, prescribed or off the shelf.

Read the label on every medicine. Warnings such as "may cause drowsiness" or "do not operate heavy machinery" apply to your car. Watch allergy and cold medicines, sleep aids, strong pain medicines and some medicines for anxiety, depression or muscle spasms. With a new medicine, ask the pharmacist or doctor whether it is safe to drive, and see how it affects you before you get behind the wheel.

Marijuana slows reaction time, makes it harder to judge time and distance, and weakens lane control. It is impairing even in states where it is legal to buy.

Alcohol plus another drug is more dangerous than either one alone. A small amount of alcohol with a cold medicine or with marijuana can impair you more than you expect.

## 11.6 Making a Plan Before You Go Out

Decide how you will get home before you start drinking, while your judgment still works.

- Pick a **designated driver** who will not drink at all.
- Use a ride service, a taxi or public transit, or stay the night.
- Call a parent or friend for a ride.
- Never ride with a driver who has been drinking or using drugs.

If you see a driver weaving or braking at random, stay well back and, when it is safe, pull over and call 911.

## 11.7 Distracted Driving

A **distraction** is anything that pulls your attention away from driving. It comes in three kinds:

- **Visual:** your eyes leave the road.
- **Manual:** your hands leave the wheel.
- **Cognitive:** your mind leaves the drive.

Texting is the worst because it is all three. Reading or typing a text takes your eyes off the road for about five seconds. At 55 mph, that is like driving the length of a football field with your eyes shut.

### Phones

Put the phone away before you start the car, and set up navigation and music while parked. If you must make a call or read a message, pull over to a safe place and stop first.

**Hands-free** is not risk free. A phone call still pulls your mind away from the road. A passenger is safer than a caller because the passenger can see the road too.

### Other Distractions

Eating, adjusting the radio or a dashboard screen, reaching for something on the floor, grooming, loud passengers, loose pets and **rubbernecking** at a crash all pull drivers off task. If something falls on the floor while you are moving, leave it until you park: a two second reach can drift you across the center line.

The crash risk for a teen driver goes up with each teen passenger in the car. Chapter 1 covers any passenger limits for new drivers.

## 11.8 Drowsy Driving

A tired driver acts much like a drunk one: slower reactions, worse judgment, drifting out of the lane. Being awake for about 18 hours impairs you about as much as a BAC of 0.05, and 24 hours about as much as 0.10. Teens need 8 or more hours of sleep a night, and most get less. A **micro-sleep** lasts four or five seconds and can come without warning. At highway speed, that is more than the length of a football field with no one steering.

Pull off the road when you notice any of these:

- Yawning again and again, or heavy eyelids.
- Not remembering the last few miles, or missing your exit.
- Drifting out of your lane or hitting a **rumble strip** (grooves at the road edge that buzz under your tires).

Drowsy crashes happen most often between midnight and 6 a.m. and in the late afternoon, when your body clock dips.

The only cure for sleepiness is sleep. Get a full night of rest before a long drive, take a break about every two hours, and share the driving when you can. If you get sleepy, pull off at a safe place, such as a rest area, and take a nap of about 20 minutes. Caffeine helps only briefly and does not replace sleep. Opening a window or turning up the music does not keep a tired brain awake.

## 11.9 Emotions, Aggressive Driving and Road Rage

A driver who is angry, upset or rushed pays less attention and takes more risks. **Aggressive driving** means unsafe moves made out of impatience or anger: speeding, tailgating, weaving, running lights. **Road rage** is anger aimed at another driver: yelling, gestures, chasing or blocking.

- Do not make eye contact, gesture back or speed up to "win."
- If someone tailgates you, move over when it is safe and let them pass.
- Keep your doors locked. Do not stop or get out to argue.
- If a driver follows you, do not drive home. Drive to a police station or a busy, well lit place and call 911.

> **Remember it:** Being right is never worth a crash. Let the angry driver go.

## Quick Review

- Alcohol affects judgment first; only time lowers your BAC.
- Adult limit 0.08; under 21, zero tolerance, and alcohol offenses can cost your license even off the road.
- Implied consent: refusing a test brings its own penalties, usually a suspension.
- Distraction is visual, manual or cognitive; texting is all three, and hands-free still distracts.
- Drowsy: pull over and rest. Angry driver: give space, call 911 if followed.
