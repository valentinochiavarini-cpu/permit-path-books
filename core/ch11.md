# Chapter 11: Alcohol, Drugs, Distraction and Fatigue

Most crashes do not start with a broken car or a bad road. They start with a driver who is not fully there: someone who had a few drinks, glanced at a phone, or kept driving after midnight with heavy eyes. Each of those drivers felt fine a second before the crash. That feeling is the trap this chapter is about.

The knowledge test asks about these topics in every state, because they cost the most lives. Drunk driving crashes kill about one person every 44 minutes in the United States. Distraction and fatigue add thousands more. The questions are not hard once you know a few numbers and a few rules, and the rules are the same idea everywhere: if your mind, eyes or hands are somewhere else, you are not driving.

Some of the details, such as the exact penalties and phone rules, are set by each state. Your state's figures appear in the boxes in this chapter. Learn the national pattern first, then the box.

> **On the test**

> - Implied consent: what happens if you refuse a breath, blood or urine test.
> - Which drugs and medicines can impair you, including legal ones.
> - What does and does not sober a person up.
> - Phone and texting rules, other distractions, drowsy driving and road rage.

## 11.1 What Alcohol Does to a Driver

Alcohol is a depressant. It slows the brain, and the brain does all of your driving. It does not wait until you feel drunk to start working. The first drink starts to dull the skills you need most.

### The Order of the Damage

Alcohol hurts **judgment** first. Judgment is your ability to make good choices: how fast to take a curve, whether a gap is big enough, whether you are fit to drive at all. That is why a person who has been drinking so often decides they are fine. The part of the brain making that decision is the part that has already been affected.

After judgment, alcohol slows your **reaction time**, the time between seeing a hazard and moving your foot to the brake. Then it weakens **coordination** (steering smoothly, staying in your lane), **vision** (focus, night vision, side vision, judging distance) and **attention** (handling two things at once, such as steering while watching a child near the curb).

| BAC | What starts to slip |
|---|---|
| 0.02 | Tracking moving objects; doing two tasks at once |
| 0.05 | Coordination, steering, emergency reactions |
| 0.08 | Concentration, speed control, spotting hazards |
| 0.10 | Staying in lane, braking at the right time |
| 0.15 | Basic control of the car and attention |

At 0.08, a driver is about four times as likely to crash as a sober driver. At 0.15, the risk is at least twelve times higher.

> **Remember it:** Alcohol steals judgment first. The drinker loses the very skill needed to notice that the other skills are gone.

### What a Drink Is

Beer, wine and liquor are not different in kind. A **standard drink** holds about the same amount of pure alcohol whatever the glass:

- 12 ounces of regular beer (about 5 percent alcohol)
- 5 ounces of wine (about 12 percent)
- 1.5 ounces of 80 proof liquor, one shot (about 40 percent)

![A regular beer, a glass of wine and a shot of liquor each hold about the same amount of alcohol.](fig/standard_drink.svg)

A large glass of wine, a strong craft beer or a mixed drink with two shots can hold two standard drinks or more. Beer is not the "safe" choice.

## 11.2 Blood Alcohol Concentration

**Blood alcohol concentration (BAC)** is the share of alcohol in your blood. A BAC of 0.08 means 0.08 grams of alcohol in every 100 milliliters of blood. Police measure it with a breath test, a blood test or a urine test.

### What Raises Your BAC

Your BAC depends on how much alcohol you drank and how fast, and also on your body:

- **Body weight and size.** A smaller person reaches a higher BAC from the same drinks.
- **Food.** Food in your stomach slows how fast alcohol enters the blood. It does not stop it.
- **Time.** Several drinks in a short time pile up faster than your body can clear them.
- **Medicines and other drugs.** These can multiply the effect of alcohol.

### Only Time Sobers You Up

Your liver breaks down alcohol at a slow, steady pace, roughly one standard drink per hour for many people. Nothing speeds that up. Black coffee, a cold shower, fresh air, exercise or a big meal after drinking do not lower your BAC. Coffee can make a person feel more awake, which is worse: now they are an alert drunk driver.

> **Test trap:** Questions often list coffee, a cold shower or exercise as ways to sober up. The only correct answer is time.

### The Legal Limit

Every state makes it illegal for a driver 21 or older to drive with a BAC of 0.08 or higher. Utah is the one state with a lower limit, 0.05. Drivers of commercial vehicles, such as large trucks and buses, are held to 0.04 under federal rules.

The limit is not a safe level. You can be arrested for impaired driving below it if the officer sees that alcohol has affected your driving. The only BAC that guarantees you are not impaired by alcohol is zero.

### Zero Tolerance Under 21

The legal drinking age is 21 in every state. For drivers under 21, every state also has a **zero tolerance** law: a much lower BAC limit, so low that a single drink can put you over it. A teen who is caught can lose their license even though an adult with the same BAC would be legal. The box above gives your state's exact under 21 limit.

## 11.3 Implied Consent and Testing

When you accept a driver's license, you agree in advance to take a chemical test (breath, blood or urine) if a police officer lawfully asks you to because they suspect impaired driving. This is called **implied consent**. Every state has an implied consent law.

Refusing the test does not make the problem go away. Refusal brings its own penalties, separate from any impaired driving charge, and in most states the first one is an automatic license suspension. The refusal can also be used against you in court.

Officers may also ask you to do **field sobriety tests** at the roadside, such as walking a straight line or following a pen with your eyes, and may use a small handheld breath tester. A blood test may be used for drugs that a breath test cannot detect.

> **Test trap:** "Can you refuse a chemical test?" Physically, yes. Legally, refusing triggers penalties, usually a license suspension. On the test, the answer that says refusing has no consequences is always wrong.

## 11.4 Impaired Driving Penalties

States call the offense by different names: **DUI** (driving under the influence), **DWI** (driving while intoxicated or impaired), **OUI** or **OVI**. The idea is the same. You may not drive while alcohol, drugs or both have affected your ability to drive safely.

The penalties are serious in every state, and they climb with each offense and with a higher BAC. Common penalties include:

- License suspension or revocation.
- Heavy fines and court costs.
- Jail time, required on repeat offenses in most states.
- An **ignition interlock**: a breath tester wired to your car's ignition. The car will not start until you blow a clean sample.
- Alcohol education or treatment programs.
- Much higher insurance costs for years.
- A criminal record that can follow you into college and job applications.

If an impaired driver hurts or kills someone, the charges can become felonies with prison time.

### Open Containers

Most states ban open containers of alcohol in the passenger area of a vehicle, for the driver and for passengers. An open container is any bottle or can that has been opened, has a broken seal, or has some of its contents removed. If you must carry alcohol, keep it sealed and in the trunk, or behind the last row of seats in a vehicle with no trunk. Your state's rule is in the box above.

## 11.5 Drugs and Medicines

Impaired driving laws cover more than alcohol. In every state, it is illegal to drive impaired by any drug: illegal drugs, marijuana, and legal medicines you bought with a prescription or off the shelf. Being allowed to take a drug is not the same as being allowed to drive on it.

### Medicines to Watch

Read the label on every medicine before you drive. Warnings such as "may cause drowsiness," "use care when operating a vehicle" or "do not operate heavy machinery" apply to your car. Common culprits:

- Allergy and cold medicines (many antihistamines cause sleepiness).
- Sleep aids.
- Strong pain medicines.
- Some medicines for anxiety, depression or muscle spasms.

If you are starting a new medicine, ask the pharmacist or doctor whether it is safe to drive, and see how it affects you before you get behind the wheel.

### Marijuana and Other Drugs

Marijuana slows reaction time, makes it harder to judge time and distance, and weakens your ability to stay in your lane. It is impairing even in states where it is legal to buy. Stimulants can make a driver take risks and then crash hard when the drug wears off.

### Mixing

Alcohol plus another drug is more dangerous than either one alone. A small amount of alcohol with a cold medicine or with marijuana can impair you more than you expect.

## 11.6 Making a Plan Before You Go Out

The decision not to drive impaired is easiest to make before you start drinking, while your judgment still works.

- Pick a **designated driver** who will not drink at all, not "just one."
- Use a ride service, a taxi or public transit.
- Stay where you are for the night.
- Call a parent or friend for a ride. Many families agree in advance: call any time, no questions that night.
- Never ride with a driver who has been drinking or using drugs. Their judgment is gone, and yours is the only one that can keep you out of that car.

If you see a driver weaving, braking at random or driving with no headlights at night, stay well back. When you can do it safely, pull over and call 911 with the location and a description of the vehicle.

## 11.7 Distracted Driving

A **distraction** is anything that pulls your attention away from driving. It comes in three kinds, and many tasks are all three at once:

- **Visual:** your eyes leave the road.
- **Manual:** your hands leave the wheel.
- **Cognitive:** your mind leaves the drive.

Texting is the worst because it is all three. Reading or typing a text takes your eyes off the road for about five seconds. At 55 mph, that is like driving the length of a football field with your eyes shut.

### Phones

The safe rule: put the phone away before you start the car. Set up your navigation and music while parked. Turn on the phone's driving mode so messages wait. If you must make a call or read a message, pull over to a safe place and stop first.

**Hands-free** is not risk free. A phone call still pulls your mind away from the road, and a driver deep in a conversation can look straight at a hazard and not see it. Talking to a passenger is safer than a phone call because the passenger can see the road too and tends to stop talking when traffic gets tense.

Nearly every state bans texting while driving for all drivers. Many states go further and ban holding a phone at all, and most states ban all phone use, even hands-free, for new or teen drivers. The rules for your state are in the box below.

> **Remember it:** Eyes, hands, mind. If a task takes any one of them off the road, it can wait until you park.

### Other Distractions

Phones are not the only problem. These pull drivers off task every day:

- Eating and drinking.
- Adjusting the radio, climate controls or a dashboard screen.
- Reaching for something that fell on the floor.
- Grooming: hair, makeup, shaving.
- Loud or rowdy passengers, especially other teens.
- Pets loose in the car.
- Looking at a crash or something interesting beside the road (**rubbernecking**).

New drivers face an extra risk from passengers. The crash risk for a teen driver goes up with each teen passenger in the car. Many states limit how many young passengers a new driver may carry; Chapter 1 covers your state's license stages.

> **Road tip:** If something falls on the floor while you are moving, leave it there. Pick it up after you park. A two second reach can drift you across the center line.

## 11.8 Drowsy Driving

A tired driver acts much like a drunk one: slower reactions, worse judgment, and drifting out of the lane. The danger is that sleep can come without warning. A **micro-sleep** lasts four or five seconds. At highway speed, that is more than the length of a football field with no one steering.

### Warning Signs

Pull off the road when you notice any of these:

- Yawning again and again, or rubbing your eyes.
- Heavy eyelids, or trouble keeping your head up.
- Not remembering the last few miles.
- Missing your exit or a sign.
- Drifting out of your lane or hitting a **rumble strip** (grooves at the edge or center of the road that buzz under your tires).

Drowsy crashes happen most often between midnight and 6 a.m. and in the late afternoon, when your body clock dips. They are often single vehicle crashes with one person in the car, at high speed, with no sign of braking.

### What Works and What Does Not

The only cure for sleepiness is sleep. Get a full night of rest before a long drive, plan a break about every two hours, and share the driving when you can.

If you get sleepy behind the wheel, pull off at a safe, well lit place, such as a rest area, and take a short nap of about 20 minutes. Caffeine helps a little for a short time, but it does not replace sleep. Opening a window, turning up the music or singing along does not keep a tired brain awake.

> **Test trap:** A question may offer "roll down the window and turn up the radio" as a fix for drowsiness. It does not work. The correct answer is to stop and rest.

## 11.9 Emotions, Aggressive Driving and Road Rage

Strong feelings are a kind of distraction. A driver who is angry, upset, rushed or grieving pays less attention and takes more risks. If you are too upset to focus, wait before you drive or let someone else drive.

**Aggressive driving** is a set of unsafe moves made out of impatience or anger: speeding, tailgating, weaving through lanes, running yellow and red lights, cutting in close after passing. **Road rage** goes further. It is anger aimed at another driver: yelling, rude gestures, chasing, blocking, or trying to start a fight.

### When Another Driver Is Angry

- Do not make eye contact, gesture back or speed up to "win."
- Give them room. Slow down or change lanes and let them go.
- If someone tailgates you, move over when it is safe and let them pass.
- Keep your doors locked. Do not stop or get out to argue.
- If a driver follows you, do not drive home. Drive to a police station or a busy, well lit place and call 911.

### Keeping Yourself Calm

Leave early so you are not racing the clock. Expect other drivers to make mistakes, and treat their mistakes as mistakes, not insults. If a driver cuts you off, ease off the gas and make space. Being right is never worth a crash.

## Quick Review

- Alcohol affects judgment first, then reaction time, coordination and vision.
- A standard drink of beer, wine or liquor holds about the same amount of alcohol.
- Only time lowers your BAC; coffee and cold showers do not.
- The adult limit is 0.08 in every state except Utah (0.05); under 21, zero tolerance applies.
- Implied consent means you have agreed to testing; refusing brings its own penalties.
- Legal medicines and marijuana can impair you, and impaired driving on any drug is illegal.
- Distraction is visual, manual or cognitive; texting is all three.
- Hands-free calls still distract your mind.
- Drowsiness is cured only by sleep; pull over and rest.
- Do not respond to an angry driver; give space and call 911 if followed.
